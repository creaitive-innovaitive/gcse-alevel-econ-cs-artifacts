Elasticities of Demand

How far does demand move when price, income or the price of another good changes? Price, income and cross elasticity of demand, with the maths, the diagrams and the exam technique.

The big idea

Elasticity measures the responsiveness of one variable to a change in another, ceteris paribus. Demand tells you the direction (price up, quantity down). Elasticity tells you by how much, as a number called a coefficient. Because it uses percentage changes, it works for any product and any currency.

Three elasticities of demand

Price (PED)

Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the product's own price.

PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP

Income (YED)

Responsiveness of demand to a change in consumers' income.

YED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔY

Cross (XED)

Responsiveness of demand for good A to a change in the price of good B.

XED = %ΔQd of A ÷ %ΔP of B

Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100. Always divide by the original value.

Sign conventionPED is negative because price and quantity move in opposite directions. Everyone ignores the minus sign, so write PED = 2.0, not −2.0, and say which way you are reading it.

What determines PED?

  • Substitutes: more, and closer, substitutes make demand more elastic. The narrower the market definition ("Brand X cola" versus "soft drinks"), the more elastic.
  • Relative expense: the larger the share of income spent on it, the more elastic.
  • Time: demand becomes more elastic in the long run as people find alternatives.
  • Necessity or luxury, addiction, brand loyalty: necessities, addictive goods and strong brands tend to be inelastic.

PED changes along a straight-line demand curve

The slope is constant but PED is not, because percentages are used. At a high price and low quantity a small absolute change is a big percentage change in quantity, so demand is elastic. At a low price and high quantity it is inelastic. At the midpoint PED = 1. Watch this happen in the next tab.

Why elasticity matters for decisions

Firms

  • Pricing: raise price on inelastic products to raise revenue; cut price on elastic ones.
  • Forecasting: YED tells a firm how sales will respond to a boom or a recession.
  • Product mix: XED shows how a rival's price change, or a complementary product, affects sales.

Governments

  • Indirect taxes: inelastic goods raise more revenue and cut consumption less.
  • Planning: YED helps predict demand for services as incomes grow.
  • Competition policy: XED can help define a market.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms so they stay true. Look up current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

Airlines and Jet Airways (India)

Fierce competition on domestic routes meant many close substitutes and low fares, so demand for any one airline is highly price elastic. When fares are cut to win passengers from rivals or from the railways, losses mount if costs are not covered. Business travellers, who must travel at set times, are less price sensitive than leisure travellers.

Exam use: a strong application for "substitutes" and for why a firm cannot raise prices without losing customers. Mention the time period: a last-minute business trip is more inelastic.

Cigarettes and tobacco taxes

Demand is inelastic because the product is addictive and has few substitutes. A tax on tobacco therefore raises a lot of revenue for the government but reduces consumption by less than the rise in price. Demand tends to become more elastic over time as people quit.

Exam use: shows why governments favour taxing inelastic goods for revenue, and why a tax may fail as a way of cutting consumption. Links to Chapter 13.

Petrol

In the short run demand is inelastic: most people still need to commute. In the long run people can buy smaller or electric vehicles, move closer to work, or use public transport, so demand becomes more elastic.

Exam use: the standard illustration of "time period" as a determinant of PED.

Luxury goods and overseas holidays

When incomes rise, spending on overseas holidays and designer goods tends to rise by a bigger percentage (YED above 1). In a recession these are cut first.

Exam use: a firm in a luxury market can forecast sales to be volatile over the economic cycle. A firm selling necessities faces steadier demand.

"Value" and own-brand ranges

Supermarket basic ranges often sell more when incomes fall. That is the behaviour of an inferior good (negative YED). As incomes recover, shoppers move to premium brands.

Exam use: use it to explain why the sign of YED matters, and why a retailer might stock more value lines during a downturn.

Printers and ink cartridges

Complements have negative XED. A fall in the price of printers raises demand for compatible ink. Some firms sell the printer cheaply and earn money on the ink.

Exam use: a clear example for cross elasticity, and for why a firm cares about the price of a complementary product.

Streaming services

Rival streaming services are close substitutes, so XED is positive and high. A price rise by one is likely to increase demand for the others.

Exam use: shows how XED helps a firm decide how closely to follow a rival's price.

Q1 Define 2 marks Define price elasticity of demand.
ReminderA definition earns marks for the measure and for what it responds to. Two marks means two ideas. Add the formula if you can.
Price elasticity of demand is a measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a product to a change in its own price, calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.
Common mistakeWriting "how much demand changes when price changes". That leaves out quantity demanded, percentage and responsiveness.
Q2 Calculate and explain 4 marks The price of a product rises from $100 to $110. Quantity demanded falls from 800 to 760 units. Calculate PED and explain what it shows.
ReminderTwo marks for the calculation, two for the interpretation. Show every step: a correct answer with no working may only score part of the marks. Divide by the original figures.
PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP. %ΔP = 10 ÷ 100 × 100 = +10%. %ΔQd = −40 ÷ 800 × 100 = −5%. PED = −5 ÷ 10 = −0.5, or 0.5 ignoring the sign. Since 0.5 is less than 1, demand is price inelastic: a 10% price rise causes a smaller, 5%, fall in quantity demanded. Total revenue rises, from $80,000 to $83,600, because the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price.
Common mistakeUsing the new price as the base, or saying "inelastic" with no comparison to 1.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain two factors that influence the price elasticity of demand for a product.
Reminder"Two factors" means two developed paragraphs. For each: name the factor (K), say which way it pushes PED (A/N), and give the chain of reasoning. Use a product as an example.

One factor is the availability of substitutes. If consumers can easily switch to close alternatives when the price rises, the quantity demanded is likely to fall by a larger percentage than the price rose, so demand tends to be elastic. A specific brand of cola has many rivals, so a small price rise may send buyers elsewhere, whereas demand for all soft drinks taken together is likely to be less elastic.

A second factor is the time period. In the short run consumers may be unable to change their habits or find alternatives, so demand is likely to be inelastic. Over a longer period they can often adapt, so demand tends to become more elastic. Petrol is likely to be inelastic for a commuter in the short run, but if prices stay high they may buy a more efficient car.

Common mistakeListing five factors in one line each. Two well-developed factors beat five names.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how knowledge of income elasticity of demand might help a clothing retailer.
Reminder"Analyse" needs a chain: what YED shows, then what the retailer does with that, then the consequence. Application to clothing keeps you in the context.

Income elasticity of demand measures how responsive demand is to a change in consumers' incomes. If a retailer sells designer clothing, YED is likely to be positive and greater than 1, so demand may rise by a larger percentage than income in a boom and could fall sharply in a recession. Knowing this, the retailer could forecast sales over the economic cycle and plan stock, staffing and finance, for example by holding less stock when a downturn is forecast to avoid unsold goods.

If the retailer also sells a basic range with low or negative YED, it may expect steadier or even rising sales in a recession, so it may shift its product mix towards those lines when incomes fall. This could reduce risk and may help the firm remain profitable over the cycle.

Q5 Assess 12 marks Assess whether a firm should reduce the price of its product to increase its total revenue.
ReminderOpen each paragraph with the question's wording. Finish each paragraph with a short evaluative point and a link back to the question. Give a final judgement that says "it depends" and then says on what.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A firm's total revenue is price multiplied by quantity sold, and its response to a price cut depends on price elasticity of demand, which is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

A firm should reduce the price of its product to increase total revenue if demand is price elastic. When PED is greater than 1, a price cut produces a larger percentage rise in quantity demanded, so the extra units sold are likely to more than offset the lower price per unit. For example, a 10% price cut with PED of 2 raises quantity by 20%, so revenue rises by around 8%. However, this depends on the firm being able to meet the extra demand; if it has no spare capacity, the price cut may only create shortages. This suggests a price cut is most likely to be sensible for a firm that faces elastic demand and can supply more.

A firm should not reduce the price of its product to increase total revenue if demand is price inelastic. When PED is less than 1, the percentage rise in quantity is smaller than the percentage fall in price, so revenue is likely to fall. In that case the firm could do better to raise price. A firm selling a necessity or a strongly branded product, such as a market-leading brand, is likely to be in this position. Even so, a firm may have objectives other than short-run revenue, such as winning market share, which could justify a cut. So a price cut is likely to be the wrong tool for a firm with inelastic demand.

Whether revenue rises also depends on the size of the price change and the time period. PED varies along a demand curve, and demand is usually more elastic in the long run, so a cut that fails to raise revenue now may work later. Rivals may also match the cut, making demand less elastic than expected.

Overall, a firm should usually cut price only if it has good evidence that demand is elastic over the range of prices involved and it can supply the extra output. If demand is inelastic it may be better to do the opposite. A key task for the firm is therefore likely to be estimating PED for its own product before deciding.

Common mistakeSaying "price cuts increase revenue because people buy more". That ignores that revenue depends on the size of the quantity response relative to the price fall.

Top tips

  • Show the formula and the working. Method marks are available even if your final answer is wrong.
  • Always divide by the original value when working out percentage changes.
  • Ignore the minus sign for PED but keep it for YED and XED, because their sign carries the meaning.
  • Compare the answer with 1. "PED = 0.5" is incomplete until you say it is below 1, so inelastic.
  • Link PED to revenue whenever a price change appears. It is the most common application.
  • Say which good, which market and which time period. Elasticity depends on all three.
  • Do not confuse elasticity with slope. A straight line has a constant slope but a changing PED.
  • Use real examples and say what the example shows about the determinant you are discussing.
