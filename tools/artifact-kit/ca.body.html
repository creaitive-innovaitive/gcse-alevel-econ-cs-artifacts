Comparative Advantage and the Gains from Trade

Why countries trade, why even the most efficient country gains from trade, how the terms of trade move, and what the theory leaves out.

The big idea

Countries have different factor endowments (land, labour, capital, enterprise, climate), so they are good at producing different things. If each country specialises in what it gives up least to make, then trades, total output rises and each country can consume more than it could produce alone.

Absolute and comparative advantage

Absolute advantage

A country has an absolute advantage if it can produce more of a product with the same resources (or the same output with fewer resources) than another country.

Example: Indonesia in rice and Brazil in coffee. It explains some trade, but not most.

Comparative advantage

A country has a comparative advantage if it can produce a product at a lower opportunity cost than another country, that is, it gives up fewer other goods to make it.

A country can have an absolute advantage in everything and still gain from trade.

Opportunity cost of good X = quantity of Y given up ÷ quantity of X gained

Worked example

With all their resources, Red can make 120 coats or 240 shirts, and Blue can make 40 coats or 160 shirts.

Why it worksFor trade to benefit both countries, the exchange rate between the goods must lie between the two opportunity costs: here between 2 and 4 shirts per coat. At 3, Red receives 3 shirts for each coat it sells, when making a coat at home only gives up 2 shirts. Blue pays 3 shirts for a coat that would cost it 4 shirts to make at home.

Benefits of specialisation and free trade

  • More output in the world from efficient use of resources; higher living standards.
  • Lower prices and more choice for consumers; cheaper raw materials and capital goods for firms.
  • Competition pushes firms to cut costs and improve quality.
  • Larger markets allow economies of scale.
  • Free trade means no taxes, quotas, subsidies or excess red tape on imports and exports.

The trading possibility curve

A country cannot produce outside its production possibility curve, but with trade it can consume outside it. In the Watch tab you will see the consumption point move beyond the PPC.

Terms of trade

Terms of trade index = (index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100
  • Rise in the index = favourable movement: export prices have risen relative to import prices, so fewer exports buy the same imports.
  • Fall in the index = unfavourable (a deterioration): more exports are needed to buy the same imports.
  • Causes: changes in demand for and supply of exports and imports, relative inflation, and the exchange rate.
  • A favourable movement is not always good: if export prices rose because costs rose, demand for exports may fall and revenue decline.
  • The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis: terms of trade tend to move against countries that export primary products, because demand for manufactures and services rises faster than demand for primary products as incomes rise.
Do not confuseThe terms of trade are about prices. The balance of trade is about values (price × quantity).

Limitations of the theory

  • Transport costs can wipe out the gain.
  • Resources are not perfectly mobile: moving workers from shirts to coats may cause structural unemployment.
  • Constant returns are assumed: doubling resources may not double output.
  • Governments may avoid overspecialisation (risky if world prices fall).
  • The exchange rate may not lie between the opportunity cost ratios.
  • Other governments may impose trade restrictions; countries often fail to adapt when comparative advantage changes.
  • In a world with many countries and goods, it is hard to identify comparative advantage.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Zambia and copper

Copper makes up more than 70% of Zambia's export earnings. Large deposits, modern mining equipment and experienced miners give it an advantage that has been built up over time. Switzerland, by contrast, is known for complex financial services and sells machinery and banking services to Zambia.

Exam use: shows factor endowments and specialisation. For evaluation, point to the risk of dependence on one commodity: a fall in copper prices hits export revenue and the terms of trade.

Peru and fishmeal

Peru has fertile fishing grounds, a long tradition and skilled workers, which give it a comparative advantage in fishmeal used in animal feed. Climate changes and labour costs relative to other fishmeal producers can change that advantage.

Exam use: comparative advantage can change over time. Distinguish it from competitive advantage, which is about cost, quality and price today.

Bangladesh and garments

Bangladesh has large supplies of labour and has specialised in labour-intensive clothing. Even if a richer country could make garments more efficiently in absolute terms, it can gain more by using its resources in sectors where its opportunity cost is lower.

Exam use: a classic case where a country with lower absolute productivity still has a comparative advantage.

Vietnam and coffee

Vietnam's climate and land suit coffee growing and the country is among the world's largest exporters. Specialising there lets Vietnam import goods that would cost it more to make at home.

Exam use: an example of climate and land as sources of comparative advantage.

Primary producers and falling terms of trade

Many countries that depend on exporting raw materials or agricultural products have seen their export prices fall relative to the prices of manufactured imports, as the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis suggests. In recent years commodity prices have also been very volatile.

Exam use: an application of terms of trade and a reason why governments may try to diversify instead of specialising fully.

Protecting steel

Where another country has gained a comparative advantage in steel, producers in the country that lost it often lobby for trade restrictions to protect jobs, rather than letting resources move to other industries.

Exam use: a limitation: governments do not always allow trade to follow comparative advantage. Link to unemployment and to the retaliation risk.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by comparative advantage?
ReminderThe key idea is opportunity cost. "Better at" or "cheaper" alone will not earn the marks.
Comparative advantage is the ability of a country to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country, that is, giving up fewer other goods to produce it.
Common mistakeConfusing it with absolute advantage (producing more with the same resources).
Q2 Calculate 4 marks Country A can produce 100 tonnes of wheat or 50 tonnes of cloth. Country B can produce 60 tonnes of wheat or 20 tonnes of cloth. Calculate the opportunity cost of a tonne of cloth in each country and state which has the comparative advantage in cloth.
ReminderOpportunity cost of cloth = wheat given up ÷ cloth gained. Show each division.
Country A: 100 ÷ 50 = 2 tonnes of wheat per tonne of cloth. Country B: 60 ÷ 20 = 3 tonnes of wheat per tonne of cloth. Country A gives up less wheat to make cloth, so Country A has the comparative advantage in cloth (and B in wheat).
Common mistakeSaying the country that makes more has the comparative advantage. Check: A also makes more wheat in absolute terms but its advantage is greater in cloth.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain two benefits to a country of specialising and trading.
ReminderTwo developed benefits, each with a chain of reasoning and a short example.

One benefit is higher output and living standards. When a country specialises in the product with the lower opportunity cost, resources are used more efficiently, so total world output rises and each country can consume more than it could produce alone. For example, Zambia concentrates on copper and imports goods it would find costly to make.

A second benefit is lower prices and greater choice for consumers. Competition from imports pressures domestic firms to cut costs and raise quality, and efficient producers abroad supply goods more cheaply. Shoppers can buy clothing from Bangladesh at lower prices than if every country produced its own.

Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a rise in a country's export prices could affect its terms of trade and its trade with other countries.
ReminderState the effect on the index first, then follow through to what it means for exports bought and for revenue. Mention that the cause matters.

The terms of trade are the ratio of export prices to import prices, multiplied by 100. If export prices rise while import prices are unchanged, the index rises: a favourable movement. Fewer exports need to be sold to buy the same quantity of imports, so the country can import more for each unit it exports.

However, higher export prices also make the goods less price-competitive abroad. If foreign demand is elastic, the quantity sold falls by more than the price rises and export revenue declines. If prices rose because of higher costs, such as wages, rather than higher demand, this is more likely. So the effect on the country depends on the cause of the rise and the elasticity of demand for its exports.

Common mistakeSaying a favourable movement is always good. It is only good if it comes from higher demand.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss two limitations of the theory of comparative advantage.
ReminderFor "discuss", explain each limitation, then add a point of evaluation on how serious it is, and conclude.

One limitation is that resources are not perfectly mobile. The theory assumes workers and capital can move freely to the industry where the country has the advantage. In practice, a coal miner cannot switch easily to making software, so specialisation can cause structural unemployment. This is more serious in the short run; over time retraining and investment can reduce it.

A second limitation is that transport costs and trade restrictions reduce the gains. If transport costs exceed the difference in opportunity costs, trade is no longer worthwhile, and tariffs or quotas imposed by other governments can block it altogether. For goods that are cheap to transport and are not heavily protected the limitation is minor.

Overall, the theory is a useful guide to why trade benefits countries, but its assumptions mean the real pattern of trade will differ from the predictions.

Q6 Assess 12 marks Assess whether a country should always specialise in the products in which it has a comparative advantage.
ReminderOpen paragraphs with the question's wording. Argue both for and against and give a judgement that says what the answer depends on.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A country has a comparative advantage in a product if it can produce it at a lower opportunity cost than other countries, and specialising means concentrating its resources on those products and trading for the rest.

A country should always specialise in the products in which it has a comparative advantage because this raises total output and consumption. By using resources where it gives up least, the country produces more in total, and by trading at a rate between the opportunity cost ratios it can consume beyond its production possibility curve. For example, Red giving up 2 shirts per coat and trading at 3 shirts per coat ends up with more of both goods. These gains depend on the terms of trade falling between the two countries' opportunity costs, and on trade being free of large barriers.

A country should not always specialise in the products in which it has a comparative advantage because over-specialisation leaves it exposed to risk. Zambia gets more than 70% of its export revenue from copper, so a fall in the world copper price would hit incomes and the terms of trade. Workers in declining industries may not be able to switch jobs, creating structural unemployment, and some countries may prefer to protect strategic industries such as food or defence. Primary producers may also face worsening terms of trade over time, as suggested by the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis.

Overall, specialising according to comparative advantage generally increases output, but a prudent government will diversify to reduce risk, invest in education to make resources more mobile, and adapt as comparative advantage changes. The best strategy therefore depends on how diversified the economy is, how easily resources can move and how stable the world prices are.

Top tips

  • Use opportunity cost, always. Comparative advantage is defined by it, not by who produces more.
  • Absolute can exist without comparative. A country can be better at everything and still gain from trade, because opportunity costs differ.
  • Keep numerical examples brief and simple. Use round numbers and add a written explanation. Check the figures show comparative advantage.
  • The exchange rate must lie between the opportunity costs. Otherwise one country does not gain.
  • Terms of trade are about prices, not values. Do not confuse them with the balance of trade.
  • A rise in the terms of trade is not always good. Ask why it rose.
  • Show both production and consumption. Trade lets a country consume outside its PPC; it cannot produce outside it.
  • Evaluate with limitations. Transport, mobility, restrictions, overspecialisation, changing advantage.
