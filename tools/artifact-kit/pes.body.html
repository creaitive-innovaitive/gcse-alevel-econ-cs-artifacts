Price Elasticity of Supply

How far producers can increase output when price rises, what limits them, and why farm prices swing so wildly while factory prices do not.

The big idea

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures the responsiveness of the quantity supplied of a product to a change in its own price, ceteris paribus. The key to PES is flexibility: the easier it is for producers to change output, the more elastic supply is.

PES = %ΔQuantity supplied ÷ %ΔPrice

Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100. The supply curve slopes upward, so price and quantity move in the same direction and PES is always positive.

Worked example

Two Bangladeshi garment makers sell at $10 and supply 100 a day. After a celebrity endorsement, price rises to $12. Producer A supplies 110, producer B supplies 140.

What determines PES?

Stocks

If firms can build up and run down stocks, they can meet extra demand quickly from stock. Services (a hotel room, an airline seat, a stadium seat) are perishable and cannot be stored, so supply is fixed in the short run: PES = 0.

Time period

In the short run, firms with spare capacity are flexible, but a shortage of key inputs (skilled workers, components, fuel) makes supply inelastic. In the long run firms can invest, adopt new technology and new firms can enter, so supply becomes more elastic.

Productive capacity

The more spare capacity, the easier it is to raise output. A factory running at full capacity cannot respond until it expands. How easily factors of production can be switched between uses also matters.

Product type

Agricultural supply is usually more inelastic than manufacturing: crops take a growing season to produce, yields depend on weather, and switching crops takes time.

Why PES matters

  • How businesses respond to changing demand. Elastic supply lets firms meet a rise in demand quickly without big price rises.
  • Price stability. When supply is inelastic, any shift in demand or supply causes a large change in price. Agricultural prices and farmers' incomes are therefore unstable.
  • Agriculture versus manufacturing. Changes in demand have a bigger effect on price in agricultural markets than in markets for manufactured goods, where PES is higher.
  • Link to taxes (Chapter 13). The less elastic side of the market bears more of an indirect tax, and gains more from a subsidy.
Do not mix upPES uses supply and quantity supplied. PED uses demand. PES is always positive; PED has a negative sign that we ignore.

Descriptions follow the coursebook and are kept general. Check current figures before quoting numbers.

Tanzania's cashew nuts

Tanzania is the world's largest producer of cashew nuts, an important source of export revenue. Supply depends on weather and on how much farmers plant, so it is unpredictable and cannot adjust quickly. The government sets an annual guide price and in a year when prices fell below the target it stepped in to buy unsold stock.

Exam use: an agricultural example of inelastic supply and volatile prices, and why governments intervene (links to buffer stocks, Chapter 13).

The price of onions in India

Most onion producers are small farmers whose only income is from selling onions. In early 2017 a bumper harvest raised supply by 11% yet wholesale prices fell by 30 to 40%. In late 2017 a poor harvest pushed wholesale prices up by 170% in a few weeks. As the saying goes: when farmers grow more they get less, and when they grow less they still get less.

Exam use: prices move far more than quantities, evidence of inelastic supply and inelastic demand. Be ready to say what extra data you would need to calculate PES (the percentage change in price that caused the supply change).

Flowers and perishable products

Cut flowers must be sold once ready. The quantity supplied on a given day cannot be varied whatever the price, so PES is zero in the very short run.

Exam use: the standard example of perfectly inelastic supply, linked to the lack of stocks.

Airline seats, hotel rooms and stadiums

Services cannot be stored. An empty seat on a flight or an unsold hotel room is lost revenue, and a stadium cannot admit more people than it has seats. Short-run supply is fixed.

Exam use: shows the "stocks" factor. When demand rises, price rises sharply (think of peak holiday flights) because supply cannot respond.

Bangladeshi garment makers

The coursebook compares two garment makers. One can increase output by only 10% when price rises 20%, the other by 40%. Differences in spare capacity, stocks of materials and flexible workforces could explain the gap.

Exam use: a numerical example for calculation and for discussing the determinants of PES.

Manufactured goods and spare capacity

A factory running below capacity can raise output quickly when demand rises, and can hold finished goods in stock. Over time, firms can build new plants or new firms can enter. That is why manufactured goods usually have more elastic supply than farm products.

Exam use: contrast manufacturing with agriculture in "why are farm prices more unstable?" questions.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by price elasticity of supply?
ReminderResponsiveness, quantity supplied, own price. Include the formula for security.
Price elasticity of supply measures the responsiveness of the quantity supplied of a product to a change in its price, calculated as the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price.
Common mistakeWriting "how supply changes when price changes". The measure is of quantity supplied, in percentages.
Q2 Calculate 4 marks The price of a product rises from $5 to $6. Quantity supplied rises from 800 to 880 units. Calculate PES and explain what it shows.
ReminderPercentage changes use the original figures. Say whether the answer is above or below 1.
PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP. %ΔP = 1 ÷ 5 × 100 = +20%. %ΔQs = 80 ÷ 800 × 100 = +10%. PES = 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5. Since 0.5 is less than 1, supply is price inelastic: quantity supplied rises by a smaller percentage than price. Producers may be unable to increase output quickly, perhaps because of limited capacity or a lack of stocks.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain two factors that influence the price elasticity of supply of a product.
ReminderTwo developed factors. For each: name it, say which way it pushes PES, explain why, give an example.

One factor is the availability of stocks. If firms can build up stocks of finished goods in advance, they can meet an increase in demand by releasing stock quickly, so quantity supplied can rise without waiting to produce more and supply is likely to be elastic. A manufacturer of tinned food can do this, but a hotel cannot store an unsold room, so its supply is likely to be inelastic in the short run.

A second factor is the time period. In the short run, firms may be unable to increase capacity or recruit skilled workers, so supply is likely to be inelastic, but in the long run they can invest in new equipment and new firms can enter, so supply tends to become more elastic. A wheat farmer may be unable to change output until the next growing season.

Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse why the price of agricultural products is usually more unstable than the price of manufactured goods.
ReminderLink: inelastic supply → a shift in supply or demand causes a big price change. Then give the reasons for the inelasticity.

Agricultural supply is generally more price inelastic than the supply of manufactured goods. Crops take a growing season to produce, farmers may find it hard to store perishable produce or switch crops, and yields depend on weather and disease, so quantity may not respond quickly to price.

When supply is inelastic, a change in demand or a bumper or poor harvest shifts the curves along a steep supply curve, so the price is likely to change by a large percentage while quantity changes little. In India, a rise of 11% in the onion harvest was accompanied by a fall of 30 to 40% in wholesale prices. In manufacturing, firms hold stocks and have spare capacity, so supply tends to be more elastic and price changes are likely to be smaller. Demand for many foods is also inelastic, which could make the price swings even larger.

Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether farmers benefit from a bumper harvest.
ReminderTwo sides. Use the idea that revenue depends on both price and quantity, and the elasticity of demand.

A bumper harvest raises the quantity farmers can sell, which could raise income if the price held up. Farmers with more crop to sell and with lower costs per unit may gain.

However, with inelastic demand for food and inelastic supply, the larger supply pushes the price down by a bigger percentage than the rise in quantity, so total revenue is likely to fall. In the onion market, supply rose by 11% while prices fell by 30 to 40%. Gains may go to consumers and processors rather than farmers, particularly for small farmers with no control over price.

Overall, a bumper harvest is more likely to reduce farmers' income if demand is inelastic, unless there is an export market, government price support or storage that stops the price from falling.

Q6 Assess 12 marks Assess whether price elasticity of supply is the most important factor in determining how much the price of a product changes when demand rises.
ReminderOpen with the question's wording. Consider PES and another factor (PED, the size of the shift, time). Judge which matters most and when.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

When demand rises, the demand curve shifts right, creating excess demand at the original price, and price rises to restore equilibrium. Price elasticity of supply measures how responsively quantity supplied increases as price rises.

Price elasticity of supply is the most important factor in determining how much the price of a product changes when demand rises if supply is very inelastic or very elastic. If supply is inelastic, firms may be unable to raise output much, so most of the adjustment is likely to come through price, which could rise sharply. If supply is elastic, output can rise more easily, so price is likely to rise only a little. Flights in the holiday season, with fixed seats, often show a big price rise; a manufacturer with spare capacity can supply more at a similar price. PES itself depends on the time period, so the same product may show a bigger price change in the short run than the long run.

However, the size of the price change also depends on the size of the shift in demand and on the price elasticity of demand. A large shift in demand could raise price a lot even if supply is fairly elastic, and a steep demand curve means that a change in quantity is likely to require a large price change. Other influences, such as government price controls or the ability to import, can also limit how far price rises.

Overall, PES is a major determinant, particularly in the short run and in markets for perishable or agricultural goods, but it is one of several factors. The judgement depends on the time period, the size of the demand shift and the elasticity of demand.

Top tips

  • Show the formula and the working. Percentage change in quantity supplied divided by percentage change in price.
  • Use the original figures as the base for every percentage change.
  • PES is positive. If you get a negative answer, one of your signs is wrong.
  • Compare the answer with 1 and use the words elastic or inelastic.
  • A straight line through the origin has PES = 1. The slope does not decide PES; the intercept does.
  • Name the factor, then say which way it moves PES. Stocks, time period, spare capacity, perishability.
  • Contrast agriculture with manufacturing whenever price instability comes up.
  • Evaluate with time. Supply is usually more elastic in the long run.
