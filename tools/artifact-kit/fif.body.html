Firm in Focus

How a firm turns inputs into output and output into profit: short-run and long-run production, the shape of every cost curve, economies of scale, revenue, and the three kinds of profit.

1

Short-run production function

In the short run at least one factor is fixed — almost always capital (the factory, the machines). Labour is the usual variable factor: the firm adjusts output by hiring more or fewer workers onto a fixed amount of kit.

total product marginal product average product law of diminishing returns fixed factor variable factor short run production function
SR vs LR production, in brief: short run = one factor fixed (usually capital), labour is varied, and the law of diminishing returns governs TP/AP/MP. Long run = no factor is fixed — the firm can rescale everything, so the question shifts from "how much labour on this plant" to "what size of plant" (isoquants/isocosts, returns to scale).

Total product (TP) — total output from a given number of workers on the fixed factor.
Marginal product (MP) — the extra output from one more worker.
Average product (AP) — output per worker, TP ÷ number of workers.

As workers are added to a fixed factory, MP rises at first (better use of kit, some division of labour) then falls — the law of diminishing returns: each extra worker has less fixed capital to share, so adds less than the one before. TP keeps rising while MP is positive, but its slope flattens; TP is at its maximum exactly where MP = 0.

Worked numbers — 1 worker: TP 10 (AP 10); 2 workers: TP 30, MP 20 (AP 15); 3 workers: TP 60, MP 30 (AP 20); 4 workers: TP 84, MP 24 (AP 21); 5 workers: TP 100, MP 16 (AP 20); 6 workers: TP 110, MP 10 (AP 18.3); 7 workers: TP 114, MP 4 (AP 16.3); 8 workers: TP 114, MP 0 (AP 14.3). MP rises up to the 3rd worker, then falls: diminishing returns set in from the 4th worker. TP keeps rising until the 8th worker adds nothing. MP cuts AP at AP's maximum, between the 4th and 5th worker. The Watch tab animates this table.

2

Short-run cost function

Costs are the mirror image of production. Because one factor is fixed in the short run, costs split into two kinds:

fixed costs (FC) variable costs (VC) total cost (TC) AFC AVC ATC MC optimum output
SR vs LR cost, in brief: short-run cost splits into fixed and variable (FC/VC), because one factor can't be changed — that split is what gives AFC/AVC/ATC/MC their shapes. Long-run cost has no fixed/variable split at all — every cost is variable, so there's just one curve, LRAC, built by choosing the lowest-cost plant size (SRAC) for each output level.
MeasureFormulaBehaviour as output rises
TCFC + VCRises throughout
AFCFC ÷ QFalls continuously — a fixed sum spread over more units ("spreading the overheads")
AVCVC ÷ QU-shaped: falls, then rises as diminishing returns bite
ATCTC ÷ Q (= AFC + AVC)U-shaped, sits above AVC by the (shrinking) gap of AFC
MCΔTC ÷ ΔQU-shaped, falls then rises faster than AVC/ATC
Why the U-shape: AVC and MC fall at first because a small workforce is under-using the fixed capital — hiring more gets more out of it. Once diminishing returns set in, each extra worker adds less output for the same wage, so the cost of producing one more unit (MC) starts climbing, dragging AVC and then ATC up behind it. The optimum output (MC = ATC) is the firm's most cost-efficient point — not necessarily its most profitable one, which depends on revenue too.
3

Long-run production function

In the long run every factor is variable — there is no fixed factor left. "Short run" and "long run" aren't fixed lengths of calendar time; they're defined by whether the firm can still vary all its inputs (installing a new factory takes longer than hiring a worker, so capital is what stays fixed while it's still "short run").

isoquant isocost long run increasing returns to scale decreasing returns to scale least-cost combination

An isoquant joins every combination of labour and capital that produces the same level of output. An isocost joins every combination of labour and capital that costs the firm the same total spend, given current factor prices — its slope is the ratio of the two factor prices. The firm's least-cost way to produce a given output is where an isocost line is tangent to that isoquant.

This is why the best combination of factors holds when the marginal product per dollar spent is equalised across every factor:

MPA ÷ priceA = MPB ÷ priceB = MPC ÷ priceC …

If a dollar spent on labour bought more extra output than a dollar spent on capital, the firm would gain by shifting spending toward labour — it will keep doing so until a dollar buys the same extra output whichever factor it lands on. That's the tangency point.

Returns to scale describe what happens to output when every factor is increased in the same proportion:

increasing returns to scale

Output rises by a larger percentage than inputs — e.g. inputs +50%, output +80%. On an isoquant map this shows as isoquants for equal output steps (100, 200, 300…) getting closer together as you move out from the origin — less extra input needed for the next chunk of output.

decreasing returns to scale

Output rises by a smaller percentage than inputs. Isoquants spread further apart — more and more extra labour and capital needed to add the same 100 units, exactly the pattern in the coursebook's isoquant map for output levels 100→500.

Joining the tangency points across a whole isoquant map traces the firm's long-run expansion path — its lowest-cost route to any output level once it can vary every factor.

4

Long-run cost function

The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve shows the lowest average cost achievable at each output level, once the firm is free to choose any scale of plant. It's built from a whole family of short-run average cost curves (SRACs) — one for each possible factory size.

SRAC LRAC (envelope curve) minimum efficient scale (MES)
Minimum efficient scale (MES) is the lowest output at which LRAC reaches its minimum — the smallest scale a firm needs to be fully cost-efficient. A low MES relative to the market (e.g. a bakery) allows many small firms to coexist; a high MES relative to the market (e.g. car manufacturing, aircraft) means only a few large firms can compete on cost, pushing the industry toward concentration.
5

Internal & external economies (and diseconomies) of scale

Internal economies come from the firm's own growth — the falling-LRAC portion of the curve above. External economies come from the whole industry or area growing, lowering every firm's costs regardless of its own size — shifting the entire LRAC curve down, not just moving along it.

TypeInternal (firm's own scale)External (industry / location)
Purchasing / input costsBulk-buying raw materials and components at a discountA cluster of suppliers nearby means every local firm gets components cheaply and fast
TechnicalLarge-scale machinery, specialisation and division of labour spread across more outputShared local infrastructure — ports, power, roads — built for the whole industry
ManagerialAffording specialist managers (finance, HR, logistics) instead of generalistsA deep local pool of skilled, industry-trained labour to hire from (e.g. Silicon Valley engineers)
FinancialLarge firms borrow more cheaply — lenders see them as lower riskKnowledge / R&D spillovers — university links, shared research, information networks
MarketingAdvertising and distribution costs spread over far more units soldReputation of a whole cluster or region (e.g. "Made in Germany" engineering)

internal diseconomies

A firm grows too large to manage well: communication breaks down, decision-making slows, workers feel alienated and less motivated. Rising LRAC beyond MES.

external diseconomies

An overcrowded industry/location pushes up costs for every firm in it — traffic congestion, bidding up local land prices, or a shortage of skilled labour as every firm competes for the same pool.

6

Total, average and marginal revenue

total revenue (TR) average revenue (AR) marginal revenue (MR) price taker price maker
MeasureFormulaMeaning
TRP × QTotal sales revenue
ARTR ÷ Q (= P)Revenue per unit sold — always equals price
MRΔTR ÷ ΔQExtra revenue from selling one more unit

A price taker (perfect competition) faces a horizontal demand curve at the market price — it can sell any quantity at that price, so D = AR = MR, all flat. A price maker faces the market's own downward-sloping demand curve: to sell more it must cut price on every unit, so MR falls faster than AR and sits below it throughout.

Revenue-maximising relationship: MR = 0 ⇔ PED = 1 ⇔ TR at its maximum. Above that point demand is elastic — cutting price raises TR (the extra units sold outweigh the lower price on each). Below it demand is inelastic — cutting price further actually lowers TR (the price cut on all units outweighs the small extra quantity sold). A firm aiming purely to maximise revenue (not profit) should produce exactly where MR = 0.
7

Normal, subnormal and supernormal profit

Profit = total revenue − total costs. But an economist's "costs" already include normal profit — the minimum return the entrepreneur needs to bother staying in this line of business at all, given what their capital and effort could earn elsewhere. It's the opportunity cost of enterprise, and it's built into TC, not paid out on top of it.

Profit typeConditionWhat it means
Normal profitTR = TC (TC includes normal profit)The firm exactly covers all its costs, including the opportunity cost of the entrepreneur's own capital and effort. Enough to keep the firm in business — no more, no less.
Supernormal profitTR > TCProfit above and beyond normal profit. Signals other firms to enter the market, chasing the same excess return.
Subnormal profitTR < TCThe firm is earning less than the opportunity cost of staying in business — a warning sign it may exit in the long run.

"If normal profit is basically covering costs, is subnormal profit actually a loss?"

In accounting terms, not necessarily — the firm can still be covering its cash costs and even turning an accounting profit. In economic terms, yes, in the sense that matters for decisions: because normal profit is already folded into TC, "subnormal" means the firm is earning less than what its resources could earn doing something else. That's a real economic loss even if the bank balance is technically positive — the entrepreneur would be financially better off redeploying their capital and labour elsewhere.

Real-world example: an independent café earning just enough to pay itself the owner's old salary

If the return exactly matches what the owner gave up (their previous job's salary plus a normal return on the capital tied up), that's normal profit — rational to keep operating, not spectacular, not a warning sign.

Real-world example: a high-street shop competing with online retailers

If it earns less than the owner could get investing that capital and time elsewhere, it's running subnormal profit — economically a loss, even if it isn't technically insolvent — and a classic reason independent shops close even while still "making money" on paper.

Supernormal profit as a market signal: new firms entering a market chasing supernormal profit is exactly the mechanism that (in a competitive market) erodes it back toward normal profit over time — supply expands, price falls, until TR = TC again.

Examples are explained in general terms so they stay true. Look up current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

A restaurant kitchen

With one kitchen and one set of ovens, the first few cooks let the team specialise and the output per cook may rise. Beyond a certain point cooks get in each other's way and queue for the same equipment, so each extra cook is likely to add less than the last.

Use it for: diminishing returns, MP below AP, and why MC rises in the short run.

Car and aircraft makers

Building a modern car or aircraft needs huge fixed investment in plant, design and testing, so the minimum efficient scale is large compared with the market. Only a handful of firms can reach it, which helps explain why these industries tend to be concentrated.

Use it for: LRAC, MES, internal economies of scale and why some markets have few firms.

Large supermarket chains

Big retailers can buy in bulk, negotiate lower prices from suppliers, borrow cheaply and spread advertising and head-office costs over a very large number of sales. Many of these cost advantages are bought through size, so smaller rivals often struggle to match their prices.

Use it for: purchasing, financial, managerial and marketing economies of scale.

Technology and manufacturing clusters

Areas such as Silicon Valley or the electronics cluster around Shenzhen have deep local supplier networks, skilled workers and shared knowledge. Every firm in the cluster may gain lower costs without growing itself.

Use it for: external economies of scale, which shift the whole LRAC curve down.

Congested city centres

As more firms crowd into one district, rents, wages and traffic delays can rise for all of them. Costs go up for every firm in the area, whatever its own size.

Use it for: external diseconomies of scale and why clusters do not grow forever.

The independent café and the struggling high-street shop

A café that earns about what the owner could have earned elsewhere is making normal profit and may reasonably carry on. A shop that earns less than the owner's best alternative is making subnormal profit, even if it still shows a small accounting surplus, and is likely to close in the long run.

Use it for: normal, supernormal and subnormal profit, and why opportunity cost sits inside total cost.

A new product that sells well

A firm with a popular new product may earn supernormal profit at first. If entry is easy, rivals could copy it, supply may rise, price may fall and profit could drift back towards normal. If there are barriers to entry, the supernormal profit may last much longer.

Use it for: supernormal profit as a signal for entry, and evaluation based on barriers to entry.

Q1 Explain 2 marks Explain what is meant by the law of diminishing returns.
ReminderTwo marks means two ideas: what happens to the factor (it is varied while at least one other is fixed) and what happens to marginal product.
The law of diminishing returns states that, in the short run, as a firm adds more units of a variable factor to a fixed factor, the extra output from each additional unit of the variable factor will eventually start to fall.
Common mistakeSaying that "total output falls". Total product may keep rising; it is marginal product that falls.
Q2 Calculate 4 marks A firm sells 500 units at $12 each. Its total cost is $5,200, which includes normal profit. Calculate its total revenue and its profit, and state the type of profit.
ReminderTwo marks for the calculations and two for the interpretation. Show the formula and the working.
TR = P × Q = $12 × 500 = $6,000. Profit = TR − TC = $6,000 − $5,200 = $800. TR is greater than TC, so the firm is earning supernormal profit of $800. Because TC already includes normal profit, this $800 is a return above the minimum needed to keep the firm in the industry.
Common mistakeCalling $800 "normal profit". Normal profit is already inside total cost.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain why a firm's short-run average total cost curve is likely to be U-shaped.
ReminderTwo developed reasons: one for the fall (spreading fixed costs, better use of the fixed factor) and one for the rise (diminishing returns). Link each to the shape of the curve.

A firm's short-run average total cost curve is likely to be U-shaped because ATC is made up of average fixed cost and average variable cost. At low output, AFC falls quickly as the fixed costs are spread over more units, and AVC may also fall because a small workforce is under-using the fixed capital, so ATC is likely to fall at first.

At higher output the law of diminishing returns is likely to set in, so each extra worker adds less output for the same wage. Marginal cost then rises, pulls AVC up, and eventually outweighs the falling AFC, so ATC rises again. A restaurant with one kitchen could show this: the first few cooks use the ovens well, but a crowded kitchen raises the cost of each extra meal.

Common mistakeUsing "economies of scale" to explain a short-run U-shape. Scale is a long-run idea; the short-run shape comes from the fixed factor and diminishing returns.
Q4 Explain 8 marks Explain how internal and external economies of scale could lower a firm's average costs.
ReminderSay who is growing in each case (the firm or the industry). Two developed points for each type, with an example, and a short comment on the difference in the curve (movement along versus shift).

Internal economies of scale are falling long-run average costs that come from the growth of the firm itself. A larger firm may buy in bulk and negotiate lower prices, so its input cost per unit could fall, and it can spread advertising and management costs over many more units. A large supermarket chain could use this to undercut smaller rivals. These economies are shown by a movement down along the LRAC curve.

External economies of scale come from the growth of the whole industry or location, not from the size of any one firm. A cluster of suppliers, a skilled labour pool and shared infrastructure may lower every local firm's costs. Technology firms in a large cluster may benefit in this way even if they are small. These economies could shift the whole LRAC curve downwards.

Both types are likely to have limits: a firm that grows too large may suffer internal diseconomies, and an overcrowded cluster may suffer external diseconomies, so the cost savings may not continue as growth goes on.

Common mistakeListing the five types of economy with no explanation of how each lowers cost per unit.
Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss whether a firm earning supernormal profit in the short run is likely to continue earning it in the long run.
ReminderOpen each paragraph with the question's wording. Finish each paragraph with a short evaluative point and a link back to the question. Finish with a judgement that depends on barriers to entry.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Supernormal profit occurs when total revenue is greater than total cost, which already includes normal profit, the minimum return needed to keep the entrepreneur in the industry.

A firm earning supernormal profit in the short run may not continue earning it in the long run if other firms can enter the market. Supernormal profit signals that resources earn more here than elsewhere, so new firms are likely to enter, supply increases, price falls and the firm's revenue per unit falls until profit returns towards normal. A popular new café format that rivals can easily copy could follow this path. However, entry may take time, so the firm could enjoy supernormal profit for several years, which means the short run is not always short in practice. Entry therefore explains why supernormal profit may disappear.

A firm earning supernormal profit in the short run is likely to continue earning it in the long run if there are barriers to entry. Patents, high minimum efficient scale, brand loyalty or control of key inputs could stop rivals entering, so supply stays restricted and price remains above average cost. A pharmaceutical firm with a patented drug may keep high profits while the patent lasts. Barriers rarely last for ever: patents expire and technology changes, so the profit may erode eventually. Barriers to entry therefore explain why supernormal profit may continue.

Overall, a firm is likely to keep supernormal profit in the long run only if barriers to entry are strong and lasting. In a market that is easy to enter, supernormal profit is likely to be competed away, so the answer mainly depends on how high the barriers are and how quickly rivals can respond.

Common mistakeWriting about perfect competition only. The question is about any firm, so barriers to entry are the key point of evaluation.

Top tips

  • Say "short run" or "long run" every time. The fixed factor changes everything: AFC, diminishing returns and the U-shape belong to the short run, economies of scale to the long run.
  • MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points. Draw it that way, never anywhere else.
  • Normal profit is inside total cost. TR = TC means normal profit, not zero profit.
  • Do not confuse diminishing returns with diseconomies of scale. Returns are short run (one factor fixed); economies and diseconomies are long run (all factors variable).
  • Internal versus external. Internal: the firm grows, a movement along LRAC. External: the industry grows, a shift of the whole LRAC curve.
  • MR = 0 is where TR is at its maximum and PED = 1. Link revenue to elasticity whenever you can.
  • Label every axis and every curve and mark the key point (optimum output, MES, the profit rectangle).
  • Use real examples and say what each one shows about the concept.
