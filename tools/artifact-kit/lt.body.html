1 · Definition

What is a liquidity trap?

A liquidity trap is a situation in which nominal interest rates are at or close to zero and people would rather hold money than bonds or spend it. Extra money supply is simply held as cash, so it cannot push interest rates lower or raise aggregate demand. Monetary policy loses its grip.

Where the idea comes from

Keynes (General Theory, 1936) argued that at very low interest rates liquidity preference becomes absolute. The demand for money curve turns horizontal, so a shift in the money supply curve changes the quantity of money but not the rate. Krugman (1998) revived the idea to describe Japan.

Why people hoard cash

Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions. When yields are very low, bond prices are high and are expected to fall when rates eventually rise. Holding bonds risks a capital loss, so people prefer cash. This is the speculative demand for money.

Why it matters

The central bank's main tool stops working just when the economy needs it. With deflation, the real interest rate (nominal rate minus inflation) can even rise while the nominal rate sits at zero, which discourages borrowing and encourages delaying purchases.

The transmission mechanism, normal and trapped

Normal times
Central bank increases money supply→Interest rate falls→Borrowing and investment rise→Consumption rises→AD rises, real GDP rises
In a liquidity trap
Central bank increases money supply→Interest rate fallsrate already at floor, extra money held idle→Investment risesweak confidence, firms repaying debt→AD rises

Two breaks in the chain: the interest rate cannot fall further (the effective lower bound), and even if it could, investment and consumption may not respond because demand and confidence are weak.

Key terms

Liquidity preference
The desire to hold wealth as money rather than as less liquid assets such as bonds.
Speculative demand for money
Money held because bond prices are expected to fall, so interest rates are expected to rise.
Effective lower bound
The level, close to zero or slightly negative, below which cutting the policy rate stops working because people can hold cash instead.
Quantitative easing (QE)
The central bank creates reserves to buy long-term assets, mainly government bonds, aiming to lower long-term yields and raise asset prices and lending.
Real interest rate
Nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate. Falling prices push it up.
Velocity of money
How many times a unit of money is spent per period. In the equation MV = PY, it collapses in a trap.
Balance-sheet recession
Richard Koo's term for firms and households repaying debt after an asset price crash, so cheap money is not borrowed.
Forward guidance
Central bank commitment about future policy, used to lower expected future short-term rates and lift inflation expectations.

Where does the money go? MV = PY

The quantity theory says nominal spending equals money times velocity. Print money, but if velocity falls by the same proportion, nominal spending does not move.

3 · Country evidence

Trap or no trap? Five economies

How to use the evidence

Japan: the long case

Rates were close to zero from the late 1990s and inflation was often negative. Firms repaid debt after the asset price crash, so cheap money was not borrowed.

Use it for: balance-sheet recession, deflation, real interest rates and the limits of QE.

The USA and UK after 2008

Rates fell to the floor and the central banks bought bonds on a large scale. Many economists think it prevented a worse slump, though the effect on lending and wages was weaker.

Use it for: evaluation of QE: it stabilised markets but may have been weaker at lifting the real economy.

Negative rates in Europe

Several central banks pushed policy rates below zero. Bank margins may have been squeezed and depositors can switch to cash, so there is limited room.

Use it for: the effective lower bound and why it is not exactly zero.

Accurate definitions and terms. Define the liquidity trap in one sentence with "interest rates at or near zero" and "money held as idle cash". Most short-answer marks live here.

AO2 Analysis

Chains of reasoning, each link explained with "which leads to". Diagrams count if labelled and referred to. Use the context: name the country, year and figure.

AO3 Evaluation

Weigh, prioritise, judge. Top-level answers say what it depends on (size of output gap, fiscal space, expectations, time frame) and reach a supported conclusion that answers the question.

Command words

Define / State
One precise sentence. No development needed.
Explain
Reason with cause and effect. Aim for two or three linked steps per mark-worthy point.
Assess / Discuss
Both sides plus a judgement. Around a third of your time should be evaluation.

Common errors

Treating zero as a hard floor
Negative rates exist (ECB, Japan, Switzerland). Say "effective lower bound", and note why it is not far below zero.
Confusing with a liquidity crisis
A liquidity crisis is a shortage of liquidity for banks. A liquidity trap is an excess of idle liquidity.
Stopping at "interest rates fall"
Always finish the chain: investment, consumption, AD, real GDP, unemployment.
Ignoring real rates
With deflation, a 0% nominal rate is a positive real rate. This is a high-value analytical point.
One-sided answers
Even "fiscal policy works in a trap" needs debt, lags and confidence as counterpoints.

Top tips

  • Say "effective lower bound", not "zero". Negative rates exist, but the room is small.
  • Finish the chain. Interest rate, investment and consumption, AD, real GDP, unemployment.
  • Use the real rate. A 0% nominal rate with deflation is a positive real rate.
  • Evaluate every policy. QE may be hoarded, fiscal stimulus raises debt, forward guidance has a credibility problem.
  • A trap is not a liquidity crisis. A crisis is a shortage of liquidity for banks; a trap is idle liquidity.
  • Use country evidence with a year and a figure.
