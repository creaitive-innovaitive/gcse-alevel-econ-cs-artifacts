The spectrum of market structures

Market structure is defined by four features: the number and size of firms, whether products are differentiated, how easily firms can enter and exit, and how much information buyers and sellers have. Those features fix how much control a firm has over price, and so what its revenue curves look like.

One rule for all four: a profit-maximising firm produces where MR = MC (with MC cutting MR from below). What differs is where MR sits. For a price taker MR is the price; for everyone else MR lies below AR, so price ends up above marginal cost.
2

Perfect competition: short run, shutdown, long run

A perfectly competitive firm takes the market price. It can sell as much as it likes at that price and nothing at a higher one, so it only chooses output. It expands until MC = MR = P. Whether that output earns supernormal, normal or subnormal profit depends on where the price line sits against ATC.

MC = MRsupernormal profitsubnormal profitshutdown pricefirm's supply curveentry and exitproductive efficiencyallocative efficiency
try this

Drag price down slowly. Profit shrinks to zero when P touches the bottom of ATC, then turns negative. The firm keeps producing while P is above AVC, because each unit still contributes something to fixed costs. Once P drops below the lowest point of AVC the firm is better off closing: it loses only FC instead of FC plus the shortfall on variable costs.

shutdown rule

Short run: stay open if P ≥ AVC (TR covers variable costs). Long run: stay only if P ≥ ATC, because every cost is variable and must be recovered.

The firm's supply curve is the part of MC above the minimum of AVC. Tick the box in the lab to highlight it.

Long-run adjustment: supernormal profit signals entry (free entry, perfect information). Market supply shifts right, price falls, and profit is competed away until P = min ATC. Subnormal profit does the reverse: firms exit, supply shifts left, price rises. The long-run equilibrium sits at the lowest point of ATC, where P = MC = min ATC, so the firm is both productively efficient (lowest cost) and allocatively efficient (P = MC).
3

Monopoly: price maker, restricted output

A monopolist faces the whole market demand curve, so selling more means cutting the price on every unit. Its MR curve therefore sits below AR (= demand). It still maximises profit at MR = MC, but then reads the price off the demand curve, not the MR curve. That gap is what creates supernormal profit and the welfare loss.

barriers to entryprice makersupernormal profitdeadweight lossnatural monopolyX-inefficiencyallocative inefficiency
compared with competition

A competitive industry with the same costs would produce where D = MC, at a lower price and a higher output. The monopolist restricts output to raise price, and the triangle between demand and MC over the lost output is the deadweight loss: units consumers value above their cost that never get made.

what monopoly can offer

Large scale can mean lower unit costs than many small firms could reach, and supernormal profit can fund R&D (dynamic efficiency). A natural monopoly has LRAC still falling at the market's whole demand, so one firm is cheapest. Regulators then cap price rather than break the firm up.

X-inefficiency: shielded from competition, a monopolist has less pressure to control costs, so its actual cost curves sit above the lowest achievable ones. Try pushing the MC slider up: the same demand now supports a higher price and less output, and the welfare loss comes from costs as well as from market power.
4

Monopolistic competition: from supernormal to normal profit

Many firms sell differentiated products (restaurants, hairdressers, clothing brands). Each has some control over price because its product is not identical to rivals', but entry is easy. In the short run a firm looks like a small monopolist. In the long run, entry pulls its demand curve left and makes it flatter until it just touches ATC.

product differentiationbrand loyaltynon-price competitiontangency solutionexcess capacity
Reading the slider: at 0% entry the firm earns supernormal profit at MR = MC. Each new rival takes away some customers, shifting D left. At 100% the demand curve is tangent to ATC at the profit-maximising output, so P = ATC and profit is normal. Past 100% (say after a demand shock) demand sits below ATC everywhere, profit is subnormal and firms leave. The tangency point is left of the lowest point of ATC, which is the excess capacity argument: firms could produce more cheaply but the demand for their particular brand is too small. The steeper the demand (strong loyalty), the more supernormal profit each firm keeps in the short run, but the tangency still holds in the long run.
5

Oligopoly: interdependence and sticky prices

With a few large firms, each firm's best move depends on what rivals do. That interdependence produces two typical behaviours: prices that stay put for long periods, and a choice between competing and colluding. The kinked demand curve explains the first.

interdependenceconcentration ratiokinked demand curveprice rigidityprice warnon-price competitioncollusioncartel

Assume the market price is P0. If the firm raises price, rivals do not follow, so it loses many customers: demand above P0 is elastic (flat). If it cuts price, rivals match to protect their share, so it gains few customers: demand below P0 is inelastic (steep). The result is a kink at P0, and a break in the MR curve directly below it. Costs can move anywhere within the gap in MR without changing the profit-maximising price.

price competition

Price cuts, promotions, price wars. Risky in oligopoly because rivals can match, leaving everyone with lower revenue and no extra share.

non-price competition

Branding, advertising, product quality, loyalty schemes, after-sales service, innovation. Hard for rivals to copy instantly, so firms compete here instead, at the cost of higher spending.

Limits of the model: it explains why price is sticky but not how P0 was set in the first place. Real oligopolies also see price leadership, where one dominant firm moves first and the rest follow.
6

Collusion and the Prisoner's Dilemma

Firms in an oligopoly can agree to keep prices high (overt collusion, a formal cartel) or move in step without an agreement (tacit collusion). Both are unstable, because each firm gains by cheating while the other holds the agreement. Game theory shows why with a payoff matrix.

game theorypayoff matrixdominant strategyNash equilibriumcarteltacit collusionrepeated games
Reading the matrix: underlined numbers are best responses. Where both players' payoffs are underlined in the same cell, neither wants to change: that is the Nash equilibrium. With the default numbers, cutting price is a dominant strategy for both (whatever the rival does, cutting pays more), so both cut and both end up with $6m, when holding would have given each $10m. The individually rational choice leaves both worse off. Lower the temptation payoff and collusion becomes stable. In a repeated game, punishment in later rounds can make holding rational, and the lab shows the probability of continuing needed to sustain it.
7

Contestable markets and X-inefficiency

A market is contestable when entry and exit are costless in practice: the threat of a rival entering matters more than the number of firms currently there. Its key feature is low sunk costs (money that cannot be recovered on exit), which allows hit-and-run entry.

effect on incumbents

Even a monopolist behaves almost competitively if entry is easy: it holds price near ATC (limit pricing), keeps costs down and takes only normal profit, because supernormal profit would attract a rival immediately. Airline routes and online retail are common examples.

when it fails

High sunk costs (advertising, specialised plant, R&D) mean a failed entrant loses everything. Incumbents can also threaten predatory pricing or use loyalty schemes, so the market becomes less contestable however many firms are present.

X-inefficiency is the gap between the cost a firm actually produces at and the lowest cost it could achieve. It appears when competitive pressure is weak: managers tolerate slack, overstaff, or overpay. The firm's cost curves sit higher than necessary. Strong competition or a credible entry threat reduces it; a protected monopoly is the textbook case where it is largest.

Examples are explained in general terms so they stay true. Look up current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

Farm produce and foreign exchange

Many small farmers selling near-identical wheat or rice, or traders in a deep currency market, come close to perfect competition: lots of sellers, a standard product, and prices set by the market as a whole. No single seller is likely to be able to change the price.

Use it for: price takers, P = MR, and why perfect competition is a benchmark rather than a typical market.

Local water and rail networks

Laying a second set of pipes or rails alongside the first would be very costly, so one provider can usually supply the whole market at lower average cost than two. These are often regulated, with price caps to stop the provider using its market power.

Use it for: natural monopoly, barriers to entry, and why regulators cap price rather than break the firm up.

Restaurants, hairdressers and clothing brands

Lots of firms sell products that are similar but not identical, and new ones can open easily. A fashionable restaurant may earn supernormal profit for a while, but rivals are likely to copy the idea and draw customers away.

Use it for: monopolistic competition, product differentiation, non-price competition and the move from supernormal to normal profit.

Supermarkets and mobile networks

A few large firms hold most of the sales. Each watches the others' prices and promotions closely, and prices often stay similar for long periods while firms compete on branding, loyalty cards and service instead.

Use it for: oligopoly, interdependence, concentration ratios, price rigidity and non-price competition.

Airline fare wars

When one airline cuts fares on a route, rivals are likely to match, and all end up with lower revenue and little change in market share. Each would be better off if all held fares, but each is tempted to cut.

Use it for: the prisoner's dilemma, Nash equilibrium, why collusion is unstable and why firms may avoid price competition.

Oil producers' agreements

Groups of producers have at times agreed to limit output to support a higher price. Each member may be tempted to produce more than its quota, which is one reason such agreements can be hard to sustain.

Use it for: cartels, overt collusion and the incentive to cheat. Say that formal price-fixing is illegal in many countries.

Budget airlines on new routes

If an incumbent airline earns high profit on a route, a low-cost rival with planes it can move elsewhere may be able to enter quickly and leave if it fails. The threat of entry can push the incumbent to keep fares closer to cost.

Use it for: contestable markets, low sunk costs and limit pricing.

Q1 Explain 2 marks Explain what is meant by a natural monopoly.
ReminderTwo marks means two ideas: the cost feature and the consequence for the number of firms.
A natural monopoly is an industry where long-run average cost keeps falling over the whole range of market demand, so a single firm can supply the market at a lower cost per unit than two or more firms could.
Common mistakeDefining it as "a monopoly that occurs naturally" or "a monopoly owned by the state". The key idea is falling LRAC across the market.
Q2 Calculate 4 marks In a market, five firms have sales of $40m, $30m, $20m, $10m and $5m. Total market sales are $150m. Calculate the five-firm concentration ratio and state what it suggests about the market.
ReminderAdd the top firms first, then divide by the total. Two marks for the method and answer, two for the interpretation.
Concentration ratio = sales of the largest firms ÷ total market sales × 100. Top five sales = 40 + 30 + 20 + 10 + 5 = $105m. $105m ÷ $150m × 100 = 70%. A five-firm concentration ratio of 70% means the largest five firms hold most of the market, which suggests an oligopoly with considerable market power.
Common mistakeDividing by the sales of the five firms instead of total market sales, which always gives 100%.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain why a firm in monopolistic competition is likely to earn only normal profit in the long run.
ReminderTell the story in order: supernormal profit, entry, the demand curve shifting, and the tangency with ATC.

A firm in monopolistic competition sells a differentiated product and faces fairly free entry, so in the short run it may earn supernormal profit where MR = MC and price is above ATC. This profit is likely to attract new firms, because entry is easy.

As new firms enter and offer similar products, they take customers from existing firms, so the demand curve facing each firm shifts left and becomes more elastic. Entry is likely to continue until demand just touches ATC at the profit-maximising output, where price equals ATC and only normal profit is earned. A new fashionable café format could follow this path once rivals copy it. If the firm has strong brand loyalty, entry may take longer and supernormal profit may last for some time.

Common mistakeSaying the firm earns zero profit. Normal profit is included in costs, so the firm covers all its costs and stays in business.
Q4 Explain 8 marks Explain, using the kinked demand curve, why prices in an oligopoly may stay the same even when costs change.
ReminderA diagram is expected. Describe the kink, the gap in MR and the effect of a cost change. Add one limit of the model for the evaluation mark.

In an oligopoly, firms are interdependent, so each must consider how rivals will react to a price change. The kinked demand curve assumes that if a firm raises its price, rivals are unlikely to follow, so demand above the current price P₀ is elastic and the firm may lose many customers. If the firm cuts its price, rivals are likely to match the cut to protect their share, so demand below P₀ is inelastic and the firm gains few extra sales.

This gives a kink in the demand curve at P₀ and a vertical gap in the marginal revenue curve below it. A rise or fall in marginal cost that stays within this gap leaves MC = MR at the same output, so the profit-maximising price is unchanged. This could help explain why prices in markets such as supermarkets can stay similar for long periods.

The model only explains why price is sticky; it does not explain how P₀ was set in the first place. Real oligopolies may also have a price leader that the other firms follow, so prices can change when the leader moves.

Common mistakeDrawing the kink but not showing the gap in MR, which is what explains the stable price.
Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss whether consumers are likely to benefit more from perfect competition than from monopoly.
ReminderOpen each paragraph with the question's wording. Finish each paragraph with a short evaluative point and a link back to the question. Finish with a judgement.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Perfect competition has many small firms, identical products, free entry and perfect information, so every firm is a price taker producing where P = MC. A monopoly is a single seller protected by barriers to entry, which faces the whole market demand curve and can choose its price.

Consumers are likely to benefit more from perfect competition because price is lower and output is higher. A competitive industry produces where demand equals MC, which is allocatively efficient, and in the long run price falls to the lowest point of ATC, so it is also productively efficient. A monopolist restricts output to the point where MR = MC and charges the higher price on the demand curve, which creates supernormal profit and a deadweight loss. A regulated water provider with no competitor could charge more without price controls. However, this comparison assumes both industries have the same costs, which may not be true in practice. On price and output, therefore, consumers are likely to be better off under perfect competition.

Consumers could benefit more from monopoly if it has much lower costs through economies of scale. If the monopoly is a natural monopoly, one large firm may have lower average costs than many small ones, and supernormal profit can fund research and development, which could give better products over time (dynamic efficiency). Pharmaceutical firms with patents may invest heavily in new drugs for this reason. But a monopoly with little competition may also suffer from X-inefficiency, so cost savings may not be passed on to consumers. Monopoly could therefore benefit consumers in some cases.

Overall, consumers are likely to benefit more from perfect competition when costs are similar, because price is lower and output higher. However, if there are large economies of scale and the monopoly is regulated or faces the threat of entry, consumers may do as well or better. The result mainly depends on how big the cost savings are and how much pressure the monopoly faces.

Common mistakeDescribing both market structures without ever answering "benefit consumers". Keep returning to price, output, quality and choice.

Top tips

  • Find the profit-maximising output first. Every structure produces where MR = MC (MC cutting MR from below). Then go up to the demand curve for the price.
  • Read the price off the demand curve, not the MR curve. This is the most common diagram error for monopoly and monopolistic competition.
  • Only perfect competition has D = AR = MR. In every other structure MR lies below AR.
  • Shade the profit rectangle between price and ATC at the profit-maximising output, and label it supernormal or subnormal.
  • Shutdown rule: short run, stay open if P is at least AVC; long run, stay only if P is at least ATC.
  • Kinked demand needs the gap in MR. Draw it and show MC moving within it.
  • Hedge the oligopoly and monopoly claims. Supernormal profit "can" persist, collusion "may" break down. Evaluate with barriers to entry and contestability.
  • Be careful with collusion. Say formal agreements are illegal in many countries, and tacit collusion is harder to detect.
