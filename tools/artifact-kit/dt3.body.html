Symmetric and Asymmetric Encryption

Why data sent over a network is encrypted, how a single shared key works (symmetric), and how a public and private key pair solves its biggest problem (asymmetric).

The big idea

Data sent over a public network can be intercepted. Encryption scrambles it into a form that means nothing to anyone who is not meant to read it. It cannot stop data being intercepted, but the interceptor gets nonsense.

2.3.1 The purpose of encryption

Whenever data crosses a public network, wired or wireless, it might be intercepted by a hacker, often called an eavesdropper. This matters most for sensitive or confidential data such as credit card or bank details, medical history and legal documents.

Plaintext

The original data before encryption. (Plaintext does not have to be text: it is just "data that has not been encrypted".)

Ciphertext

The encrypted data that comes out of the encryption algorithm.

Encryption algorithm

The method that scrambles the plaintext. It uses an encryption key to produce the ciphertext.

Key

A value used by the algorithm. Without the correct decryption key the data cannot be unscrambled.

The route of an encrypted message

plaintext → encryption algorithm + encryption key → ciphertext → (internet) → ciphertext → decryption algorithm + decryption key → plaintext

2.3.2 Symmetric encryption

In symmetric encryption the same key is used to encrypt and to decrypt.

A simple example uses a 10-digit key such as 4 2 9 1 3 6 2 8 5 6. Each letter is shifted along the alphabet by the next digit of the key (+4, +2, +9, +1, and so on). The receiver applies the same key in reverse (−4, −2, −9, −1 …).

  • A 10-digit key has only 10¹⁰ possible values, which a modern computer could try in seconds. Real systems use keys of 256 bits, giving 2²⁵⁶ (about 1.2 × 10⁷⁷) possible keys.
  • The real difficulty is keeping the key secret. Both sides need the same key, so it has to be passed to the other person somehow, for example in an email or text message, where it can be intercepted.
The main drawbackIf the key is intercepted, the eavesdropper can decrypt everything. This is the key distribution problem: security of the shared key.

Asymmetric encryption

Asymmetric encryption was developed to solve that problem. It uses two linked keys:

Public key

Made available to everybody. Used to encrypt a message to its owner.

Private key

Known only to its owner. Used to decrypt messages that were encrypted with the matching public key.

The two keys are mathematically linked, but one cannot be worked out from the other. Both are needed: one to encrypt, the matching one to decrypt.

Tom sends Jane a confidential document

  1. Jane uses an algorithm to generate a matching pair of keys (public and private) and keeps them on her computer.
  2. Jane sends her public key to Tom. (Anyone may have it.)
  3. Tom uses Jane's public key to encrypt the document, and sends the ciphertext to Jane.
  4. Jane uses her matching private key to decrypt it. She cannot decrypt it with the public key.

Jane can give her public key to many people, so she can receive encrypted messages from all of them, and she decrypts each with the one private key she never shares.

Two-way communication

At the moment Jane can only receive encrypted documents from Tom. For Jane to send encrypted documents back to Tom, Tom must also generate his own public and private key pair, and send his public key to Jane. If five colleagues all want to exchange encrypted messages, each needs their own key pair and each must share their public key with all the others.

Comparing the two

Extension: quantum cryptography

This is an A Level extension in the course book. It is not needed for the IGCSE exam.

Quantum cryptography uses the physics of photons (particles of light) rather than mathematics to send encryption keys securely. A protocol called Quantum Key Distribution (QKD), such as BB84, lets two people share a key. The sender and receiver each choose a measuring method (basis) at random for every photon, and then publicly compare which they used. They keep only the results where both used the same basis. A spy who measures the photons disturbs them, which is why the method is thought to be very secure.

Descriptions are kept general. Check current details before quoting specifics.

The padlock in your browser

When a web address begins https and the browser shows a padlock, the data between your device and the website is encrypted. A card number typed into a shop's page is scrambled on the way, so someone sharing the same Wi-Fi sees only ciphertext. In practice, systems like this tend to use public and private keys to agree a shared key first, and then use that shared key for the bulk of the data. You only need the two basic types for the exam.

Exam use: a standard reason for encryption: protecting card details on a public network.

Messaging apps

Many messaging apps advertise "end-to-end encryption": the message is encrypted on the sender's device and only decrypted on the receiver's device, so the company running the network in between cannot read it.

Exam use: shows that encryption protects data even when it passes through other people's networks.

Home and café Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi signals travel through the air, so anyone nearby can pick them up. Encrypted Wi-Fi turns the signal into ciphertext for anyone without the key. Unencrypted public Wi-Fi leaves eavesdroppers with readable data.

Exam use: a wireless example. Eavesdroppers can intercept data either way, but encryption makes it meaningless.

Online banking and health records

Bank logins and medical records are exactly the sort of sensitive data the course book mentions. Both are encrypted when sent so that a stolen copy is of no use.

Exam use: examples of confidential data: credit card or bank details, medical history, legal documents.

Giving out your public key

Some people publish their public key on a website or in their email footer so anyone can send them an encrypted message. Their private key stays on their own device and is never shared.

Exam use: illustrates "public key: made available to everybody; private key: known only to the owner".

Exam-style questions

Open each question for reminders, a model answer and common mistakes. Each underlined phrase earns one mark; the number shows which mark point it is.

Q1 State 2 marks What is meant by encryption?
ReminderMake data unreadable, and say who can read it again.
Encryption alters (scrambles) data into a form that cannot be understood by anyone it was not intended for, using an encryption key; it can only be decoded using the correct decryption key.
Common mistakeSaying "it stops hackers". Encryption does not stop data being intercepted.
Q2 Name 2 marks Name (a) the data before it is encrypted and (b) the data after it is encrypted.
ReminderTwo words from the chapter.
(a) Plaintext (b) Ciphertext
Common mistakeWriting "simpletext" or "encrypted text". Use plaintext and ciphertext.
Q3 Explain 3 marks Explain why data is encrypted before it is sent across a public network.
ReminderInterception risk, what encryption does and does not do, and an example of sensitive data.
Data sent over a public network could be intercepted by a hacker (an eavesdropper). Encryption does not stop the data being intercepted, but it turns it into ciphertext that makes no sense without the decryption key. This protects sensitive or confidential data, such as bank or credit card details, medical history or legal documents.
Common mistakeSaying the hacker "cannot get" the data. They can get it, but they cannot read it.
Q4 Describe 3 marks Describe symmetric encryption and give one drawback.
ReminderOne key used at both ends. Then the drawback.
The same key is used to encrypt the data and to decrypt it. The sender and the receiver must both have a copy of the key. Drawback: the key must be sent to the receiver, and it could be intercepted on the way, so the security of the key is a problem.
Common mistakeSaying the drawback is that it is "too slow" or "too complicated". The textbook drawback is key security.
Q5 Describe 4 marks Tom wants to send Jane a confidential document using asymmetric encryption. Describe the steps.
ReminderKeys generated, public key shared, encrypt with the public key, decrypt with the private key.
Jane generates a matching pair of keys, a public key and a private key. Jane sends her public key to Tom. Tom encrypts the document using Jane's public key and sends the ciphertext to Jane. Jane decrypts the document using her matching private key.
Common mistakeSwapping the keys. Tom encrypts with Jane's public key; Jane decrypts with her private key.
Q6 Explain 3 marks Explain why asymmetric encryption is more secure than symmetric encryption.
ReminderWhat has to be sent, and what an eavesdropper could do with it.
In symmetric encryption the secret key has to be sent to the receiver, where it could be intercepted. In asymmetric encryption only the public key is sent, and the private key is never shared; it stays with its owner. If the public key is intercepted, it can only encrypt, not decrypt, so the eavesdropper cannot read the messages.
Common mistakeSaying it is more secure "because it has two keys". Explain what travels and what does not.
Q7 State 2 marks A student says "Encryption stops hackers from intercepting my data." Is the student correct? Explain.
ReminderOne mark for the verdict, one for what encryption really does.
No, encryption cannot prevent data being intercepted. It makes the intercepted data meaningless (ciphertext) to anyone without the decryption key.
Common mistakeAgreeing with the student. This is a very common misconception.
Q8 State 2 marks In asymmetric encryption, state who has access to (a) the public key and (b) the private key.
ReminderOne word for each: everybody, or one person.
(a) The public key is available to everybody (it can be shared with anyone). (b) The private key is known only to its owner (the receiving user).
Common mistakeSaying the private key is "sent to the receiver". It is the receiver's own key; it never travels.
Q9 Calculate 2 marks A simple cipher shifts each letter along the alphabet by the next digit of the key 2 4 1, repeating the key as needed. Encrypt the word COMPUTER.
ReminderC + 2, O + 4, M + 1, then the key starts again.
Shifts used: 2 4 1 2 4 1 2 4 (C+2 = E, O+4 = S, M+1 = N, P+2 = R, U+4 = Y, T+1 = U, E+2 = G, R+4 = V) Ciphertext: ESNRYUGV
Common mistakeUsing the whole key for each letter, or forgetting to restart the key.

Top tips

  • Encryption does not stop interception. It makes intercepted data meaningless.
  • Plaintext → algorithm + key → ciphertext. Use those exact words.
  • Symmetric = one key, both ends. Its drawback is that the key has to be sent securely.
  • Asymmetric = two keys. Public: everybody. Private: only the owner.
  • Encrypt with the receiver's public key. Decrypt with the receiver's private key.
  • Say why asymmetric is more secure: the private key never travels, and a stolen public key cannot decrypt.
  • Two-way needs two pairs. Both people need their own key pair.
  • Give a reason with an example: credit card details, medical history, legal documents.
