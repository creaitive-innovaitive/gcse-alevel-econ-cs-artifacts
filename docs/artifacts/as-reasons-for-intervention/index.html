Reasons for Government Intervention in Markets

Why markets fail to supply public goods, over-consume demerit goods and under-consume merit goods, and why governments set maximum and minimum prices.

The big idea

When markets work well, the price mechanism allocates scarce resources efficiently. Market failure is an inefficient allocation of resources: the price mechanism fails to take account of all the costs and benefits of producing or consuming a product. That gives governments a reason to intervene. This chapter is about the reasons. The methods (taxes, subsidies and so on) are in Chapter 13.

Four situations of market failure

Public goods

Not provided by the free market.

Merit goods

Under-consumed (and under-provided).

Demerit goods

Over-consumed (and over-provided).

Information failure

Consumers lack the information they need to decide well.

1 · Non-provision of public goods

Public goods are goods like street lights, national defence, police, fire protection, non-toll roads and flood control. They have two characteristics:

  • Non-excludable: once provided, nobody can be stopped from using them.
  • Non-rival: one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others.

This creates the free rider problem: people can enjoy the good without paying for it. A private firm cannot charge, so it cannot make a profit, so it will not supply it, even though the benefits to society exceed the cost. The government funds public goods from tax revenue and provides them free. The opportunity cost is that the money could be spent elsewhere, especially in countries with a modest tax base.

Best exampleA lighthouse matches both characteristics exactly: every ship can see the light, and one ship seeing it does not use it up.

2 · Over-consumption of demerit goods

Demerit goods, such as cigarettes, harmful drugs and in some views sugary foods, are considered undesirable for consumers. In a free market they are over-provided and over-consumed, mainly because consumers lack full information about the harm. Governments intervene with bans on smoking in public places, graphic health warnings and regulation. Reasons: protect health, promote a more productive workforce, and save healthcare spending. These matter more than the lost tax revenue from lower sales.

3 · Under-consumption of merit goods

Merit goods, such as healthcare and education, are more beneficial than consumers realise (information failure), and often give benefits to others too. If left to the private sector, too little is supplied, and access is limited to those who can afford it.

In the coursebook's healthcare diagram, the government believes quantity OY is needed, but the private sector provides only OX, leaving under-provision of OY − OX. The government can take over provision entirely, or let the private sector continue and fill the gap for those who cannot pay. Education is also a human right, and governments often make it compulsory.

4 · Why governments control prices

Maximum prices (price ceilings)

A legal limit on how high a price can go. To bite, it must be below the equilibrium price.

  • Protect low-income households by making basic items such as bread affordable.
  • Rent controls provide affordable housing and attract key workers such as nurses and teachers to expensive areas.

Minimum prices (price floors)

A legal limit on how low a price can go. To bite, it must be above the equilibrium price.

  • Support farmers' incomes, which are unpredictable because supply is uncertain.
  • Protect food supplies: governments want to rely less on imports for items like wheat and rice.

Downsides: a minimum price makes consumers worse off, since price is higher and less is traded, and it can be an inefficient use of resources. A maximum price leads to shortages (see Chapter 13).

Reasons, not methodsIf the question asks why a government intervenes, explain the market failure. Save taxes, subsidies and provision for questions about how.

6 · Multiple choice

Descriptions follow the coursebook and are kept general. Check current details before quoting numbers.

Education in Sub-Saharan Africa

Education is a merit good and would be under-provided if left to the private sector. In most low and lower middle-income countries, governments provide education directly or subsidise it, and may make it compulsory. Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest rates of children not in education, mainly because of poverty. Kenya has made government education free from primary to high school, with subsidies for boarding fees for low-income families.

Exam use: an example of a merit good, information failure and positive benefits for others. Evaluate with opportunity cost and funding limits.

Healthcare reform in Pakistan

Pakistan's government has promised to reform healthcare so that quality care is available to all, not just those who can pay private providers. The main issues are funding (with a limited tax base), building hospitals in rural areas, preventative care such as immunisation, and regulating private and state providers.

Exam use: healthcare as a merit good, with real constraints on funding and opportunity cost.

Smoking bans and graphic warnings

Many countries ban smoking in public places and require written warnings and graphic photographs on packaging. Governments justify this to protect health, promote a more productive workforce and cut healthcare spending. These arguments have usually outweighed the loss of tax revenue from falling tobacco sales.

Exam use: cigarettes are the standard demerit good, with information failure as the reason.

Lighthouses, street lighting and flood defences

These are classic public goods. Everyone nearby benefits and nobody can be stopped from benefiting, so private firms cannot charge and the government pays from taxation.

Exam use: a lighthouse is the clearest example of both characteristics of a public good.

Bread price ceilings

In some former socialist economies, basic foods such as bread were given a maximum price to protect low-income households. The price was held below the market level, and shortages and queues were common.

Exam use: a maximum price example. State the aim (affordability) and be ready to explain the consequences.

Rent controls for key workers

Rent controls aim to provide affordable housing for low-income families and to attract key workers such as nurses and teachers to areas with high housing costs.

Exam use: an aim for a maximum price other than "low income". Link to shortages in Chapter 13.

Wheat and rice price floors

Many governments support farmers' incomes with a minimum price for key crops such as wheat and rice. Farm incomes are unpredictable because supply is uncertain, and governments also want to keep food supplies secure.

Exam use: a minimum price example. Evaluate: farmers gain, consumers pay more and it can be an inefficient use of resources.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by a merit good?
ReminderSay what it is and why the market under-provides it.
A merit good is a good that is more beneficial to consumers than they realise, so it tends to be under-consumed and under-provided in a free market, such as healthcare or education.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain why the private sector would not provide street lighting.
ReminderUse both characteristics and the free rider problem. End with the consequence for supply.
Street lighting is a public good: it is non-excludable, so nobody can be stopped from using it, and non-rival, since one person's use does not reduce what is available to others. This creates the free rider problem, because people can benefit without paying. A private firm may be unable to charge for it, so it could struggle to earn revenue to cover its costs and make a profit. The firm is therefore unlikely to supply it, so the government often provides it, paid for out of taxes.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain why cigarettes are over-consumed in a free market.
ReminderThe reason is information failure. Say who lacks what.
Cigarettes are a demerit good: they are harmful to the consumer and considered undesirable. Consumers often lack full information about the health effects, or underestimate them, and the product is addictive, so they may buy more than they would if they knew the true costs. Quantity consumed is likely to be higher than is best for society, which is why governments use health warnings and smoking bans.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse why a government might set a minimum price for an agricultural product.
ReminderReasons first (income stability, food supplies), then the effect on the market, then a consequence.

A minimum price is a legal floor set above the equilibrium price. Agricultural prices and farmers' incomes are unpredictable because weather and disease make supply unpredictable, so a floor can give farmers a more stable and higher income. It may also encourage domestic production, which could reduce the need to import and make food supplies more secure.

Governments in many countries set minimum prices for staple crops such as wheat and rice. At the higher price, quantity supplied rises and quantity demanded falls, which is likely to create a surplus, and consumers pay more, so there is an opportunity cost.

Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether governments should set maximum prices for essential goods such as bread.
ReminderArguments for and against, then a judgement. Remember the control only works if it is below equilibrium.

Governments might set maximum prices on essential goods to protect low-income households, helping to make sure they can afford basic items. In some economies, bread was given a ceiling for this reason. If the price is held below the equilibrium, consumers who can buy the good pay less.

However, a lower price reduces the incentive to supply while raising the quantity demanded, so there is likely to be a shortage. Goods may have to be rationed, queues could form and black markets can appear at higher prices. Suppliers may cut quality or leave the market, and the people the policy aims to help may be the ones who cannot find the product.

Overall, a maximum price can help in the short run, especially in emergencies, but is likely to cause problems if maintained. Targeted help such as a subsidy or a cash transfer may be a better way of protecting the poor without creating a shortage.

Q6 Assess 12 marks Assess whether government intervention is justified to correct the under-consumption of healthcare.
ReminderStart with why healthcare is a merit good. Balance the case for intervention with its costs. Give a judgement.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Healthcare is a merit good: it is more beneficial to consumers than they realise because of information failure, so a free market is likely to under-provide it and may give access mainly to those who can pay.

Government intervention is often justified to correct the under-consumption of healthcare because the market quantity is lower than the socially desirable quantity. If the private sector supplies only OX when society needs OY, the government can provide or fund the difference, so that those who cannot afford fees still receive care. In Pakistan, reform has been aimed at giving quality care to all and not only to those who can pay private providers. A healthier population is likely to be more productive, which can support economic growth and development. This argument is strongest where there are large benefits to others, such as preventing the spread of disease through immunisation.

Intervention may not be justified, or may be less effective, because of its cost and the way it is delivered. Healthcare funded by taxes has an opportunity cost, since money spent on hospitals cannot be spent on other things, particularly in countries with a limited tax base. Free provision can also lead to excess demand, long waiting times and inefficiency if there is no competition. Government may lack the information to decide how much is needed, which is government failure.

Overall, intervention is likely to be justified because the benefits of healthcare reach beyond the individual and many people may not be able to afford it otherwise. The extent should depend on the country's budget and the efficiency with which it is spent. A mixed approach, with government funding the core services and private providers adding capacity, may achieve most of the benefits at the lowest cost.

Top tips

  • Define market failure properly. An inefficient allocation of resources because the price mechanism ignores some costs or benefits.
  • Use both characteristics of a public good. Non-excludable and non-rival, then the free rider problem.
  • Information failure is the key reason behind merit and demerit goods. Say who lacks what.
  • Under-consumption for merit, over-consumption for demerit. Do not swap them.
  • A price control only bites on the right side of equilibrium. Maximum below, minimum above.
  • Give a reason (the "why") before the method. This chapter is about reasons.
  • Evaluate with opportunity cost. Government funds are limited, especially where the tax base is small.
  • Use examples: lighthouse, healthcare, education, cigarettes, rent controls, wheat and rice.
