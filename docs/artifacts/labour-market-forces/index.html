Labour Market Forces and Government Intervention

How wages and employment are decided: the demand for labour (marginal revenue product), the supply of labour, wage differentials, monopsony, trade unions, and what a minimum wage does in each case.

The big idea

A wage is the price of labour, set where the demand for labour meets the supply of labour. Firms demand labour because of what workers help them sell, so the demand is a derived demand. Workers supply labour by trading off pay against leisure. The government can change the outcome with a minimum wage, and the effect depends on whether the labour market is competitive or has a monopsony employer.

The demand for labour

Marginal revenue product (MRP)

The extra revenue a firm gains from employing one more worker. It is the marginal physical product of the worker multiplied by the marginal revenue from each extra unit sold.

MRP = MPP × MR

If the firm is a price taker in the product market, MR is the price, so MRP = MPP × P.

The hiring rule

A profit-maximising firm keeps hiring while each extra worker adds more to revenue than to cost. In a competitive labour market the extra cost of a worker is the wage, so the firm hires up to the point where:

MRP = wage (MC of labour)

The firm's MRP curve slopes downward because of diminishing marginal returns, so it is the firm's demand curve for labour.

Why derived?Nobody wants a worker for the sake of it. A firm wants the output that labour helps to make. If demand for the product rises, the demand for the labour that makes it tends to rise too.

Wage elasticity of demand for labour

This measures how responsive the quantity of labour demanded is to a change in the wage: %Δ quantity of labour demanded ÷ %Δ wage. Demand is likely to be more elastic when:

  • labour is a large share of total costs, so a wage rise changes costs a lot (a small share makes demand less elastic);
  • there are good substitutes for labour, such as machines, and they can be introduced easily;
  • the demand for the product is elastic, so higher costs passed on in prices cut sales and jobs;
  • the time period is long, because firms have time to change their methods of production.

The supply of labour

The individual worker

When the wage rises two effects pull in opposite directions. The substitution effect: leisure becomes more expensive, so the worker may supply more hours. The income effect: the worker earns more per hour and may choose to buy more leisure and work fewer hours.

At low wages the substitution effect usually dominates. At high wages the income effect may dominate, which gives a backward-bending supply curve.

The market (an occupation)

Higher wages in one occupation attract workers from other occupations and from outside the labour force, so the supply curve for a particular occupation slopes upward.

Supply is more elastic when skills are easy to acquire, when there is spare labour (unemployment) and over a longer time. It is less elastic for jobs needing long training, such as surgeons.

What shifts the supply of labour?

  • Changes in population, migration and the age structure of the workforce;
  • Changes in wages in other occupations and in non-wage benefits (hours, holidays, job satisfaction);
  • The cost and length of training, and the availability of education;
  • Changes in social attitudes and legislation, for example the participation of women or the retirement age.

Equilibrium, wage differences and economic rent

In a competitive market the wage settles where demand equals supply. Wages differ between jobs because the demand and supply conditions differ:

  • Skills and training: jobs needing scarce skills have a limited supply and often a high MRP.
  • Compensating differences: unpleasant, risky or unsocial jobs may pay more to attract workers.
  • Productivity and MRP: workers who add more to revenue tend to be paid more.
  • Imperfections: discrimination, poor information, trade union power and geographical or occupational immobility can keep wages different from the competitive level.

Transfer earnings

The minimum payment needed to keep a worker in their current job: what they could earn in their next best alternative.

Economic rent

Any payment above transfer earnings. It is larger when supply is inelastic (a very scarce talent) and smaller when supply is elastic.

Economic rent = actual earnings − transfer earnings

Monopsony

A monopsony is a market with a single buyer of labour. In practice it means an employer with wage-setting power, such as the main hospital employer in a region or a big firm in a small town.

Why MCL is above the supply curve

The supply curve is the average cost of labour (ACL): the wage each worker gets. To hire one more worker the monopsonist must raise the wage, and (if it cannot pay different workers different wages) it must pay that higher wage to all its workers. So the marginal cost of labour (MCL) lies above the ACL.

The outcome

The firm hires where MCL = MRP, but pays the wage on the supply curve for that quantity. Compared with a competitive market, employment is lower and the wage is lower, and the wage is below the worker's MRP.

CarefulRead across from MCL = MRP to the supply (ACL) curve to find the wage. The most common error is to read the wage off the MCL or MRP curve.

Trade unions

A trade union is an organisation of workers. It may bargain collectively over pay and conditions, which can lift wages above the competitive level.

  • In a competitive market a wage above equilibrium is likely to reduce employment (the same effect as a minimum wage), unless the union also raises productivity.
  • Against a monopsony a union can offset the employer's power: this is a bilateral monopoly, and the bargain may raise the wage towards the competitive level without cutting employment.
  • Insiders and outsiders: higher wages for members may come at the cost of jobs for those outside the union.
  • Unions can also improve working conditions, training and productivity, and cut staff turnover.

Government intervention: the minimum wage

Competitive labour market

A binding minimum wage is set above the equilibrium wage. The quantity of labour supplied rises and the quantity demanded falls, so there is unemployment. Workers who keep their jobs gain; those who lose them, or cannot find one, lose.

Monopsony

A minimum wage that lies between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage can raise both the wage and employment. The firm can no longer cut the wage to hire fewer people, so its marginal cost of labour is flat at the minimum wage until supply runs out.

Evaluating a minimum wage

  • The size of the minimum: a low minimum may not bind; a very high one is more likely to cost jobs.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: the less elastic the demand, the smaller the loss of jobs.
  • Market structure: real labour markets are rarely perfectly competitive, so job losses may be smaller than the simple model suggests.
  • Who gains: it may reduce poverty and inequality, but low-paid workers in households that are not poor also benefit, and some workers may lose jobs or hours.
  • Other effects: higher productivity and morale, lower turnover and higher consumer spending may offset the cost; firms may raise prices, cut hours or replace workers with machines.
  • Time period: the effects may differ between the short run and the long run.

The UK National Minimum Wage and the Low Pay Commission

The UK introduced a national minimum wage in 1999 and a higher rate for workers aged 25 and over, the National Living Wage, in 2016. An independent body, the Low Pay Commission, advises on the rates. Many studies found small effects on total employment when the wage was introduced, though the effects of larger rises are debated.

Exam use: evidence for evaluation. The job losses predicted by the simple competitive model were small, which suggests many low-wage markets have some monopsony power or that demand for labour is inelastic.

Fast food in New Jersey and Pennsylvania

In the 1990s the economists David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food employment in New Jersey, which raised its minimum wage, with neighbouring Pennsylvania, which did not. They did not find the fall in employment the competitive model would predict.

Exam use: a well-known study that supports the monopsony view. Evaluate by noting that other researchers disputed the methods and results.

Nurses and the NHS

In many areas the National Health Service is by far the largest employer of nurses. Pay is largely set nationally rather than by local competition, and nurses have few other employers who need their specific skills.

Exam use: an example of a monopsony buyer of a specialised labour supply. Pay rises that increase the number of nurses are consistent with the monopsony model.

Footballers and economic rent

The best Premier League players earn many times what they could in any other job. Their talent is scarce, so supply is highly inelastic and most of their pay is economic rent.

Exam use: a clear case of large economic rent. Write the formula: earnings minus transfer earnings.

Surgeons and compensating differences

Surgeons earn high wages because the training takes many years and costs a lot, so supply is restricted, and because the MRP of their skills is high.

Exam use: use for wage differentials: scarce skills, long training and a high MRP.

Trade unions and train drivers

Rail unions in several countries negotiate pay for train drivers, whose absence can stop a whole network. That gives the union bargaining power because the demand for their labour is inelastic and substitutes are hard to find.

Exam use: union power depends on elasticity of demand for the labour. Link it to the costs of strikes for consumers and firms.

Gig economy platforms

Ride-hailing and delivery platforms can set the rate paid to a large number of drivers and couriers, who may have few alternatives in their area. Some economists see this as an example of monopsony power.

Exam use: a modern application of wage-setting power. Evaluate: drivers can often work for more than one platform, which weakens the monopsony case.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by the marginal revenue product of labour?
ReminderSay what it is per worker and where the revenue comes from.

Q2 Calculate 3 marks A firm sells its product at $5 a unit. The 4th worker adds 6 units of output. The wage is $24 per day. Calculate the MRP of the 4th worker and state whether the firm should employ that worker.
ReminderIn a competitive product market MR is the price. Compare MRP with the wage.
MRP = MPP × MR = 6 × $5 = $30. The MRP of $30 is greater than the wage of $24, so the firm should employ the 4th worker, because the extra revenue ($30) is more than the extra cost ($24) and profit rises by $6.
Common mistakeStopping at $30 without comparing it with the wage.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain why a monopsonist may pay workers less than their marginal revenue product.
ReminderTwo links: MCL above the supply curve, then the wage is read off the supply curve.

A monopsonist is the only buyer of labour, so it faces the whole upward-sloping supply curve and must pay a higher wage to attract more workers. If it pays that higher wage to all its workers, the marginal cost of labour is above the wage, so it is likely to hire fewer workers, where MCL equals MRP. It then pays the wage that is just enough to attract that number of workers, read from the supply curve, which lies below MRP at that level of employment. For example, a hospital that is the main employer of nurses in a region may be able to pay below their MRP.

Common mistakeSaying the wage is where MCL meets MRP. That is the quantity; the wage is read down to the supply curve.
Q4 Analyse 8 marks Analyse the likely effects of introducing a minimum wage above the equilibrium wage in a competitive labour market.
ReminderDraw the diagram first: label the minimum wage, the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied, and the gap. Then describe winners and losers.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A minimum wage is a legal floor on the wage firms may pay. If it is set above the equilibrium wage, the higher wage is likely to make some firms demand fewer workers, because each worker now costs more than their marginal revenue product, so the quantity of labour demanded may fall.

At the same time the higher wage may attract more people into work, so the quantity of labour supplied may rise. Supply then exceeds demand and the result could be unemployment, equal to the gap between the two quantities. For example, if the equilibrium wage is $12 and the minimum is $14, firms might demand 60,000 workers while 100,000 want jobs, leaving 40,000 unemployed.

Workers who keep their jobs may be better off, but those who lose them or cannot find work may be worse off, and firms' costs rise, which may lead to higher prices or machines replacing workers. The size of the job losses is likely to depend on how elastic the demand for labour is: if it is inelastic, fewer jobs may be lost.

Common mistakeDrawing the minimum wage below equilibrium: it would have no effect. It must be set above.
Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss whether a national minimum wage is likely to benefit low-paid workers.
ReminderArgue both sides with diagrams, then give a judgement that says what the answer depends on: the level of the minimum, market structure and the elasticity of demand for labour.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A national minimum wage is a legal minimum hourly pay that applies across the economy, and low-paid workers are those whose earnings are in the lowest part of the distribution.

A minimum wage is likely to benefit low-paid workers who stay in work, because it raises their hourly pay. If an employer has monopsony power and pays below MRP, a minimum wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage could raise both the wage and employment, as the firm can no longer cut the wage to hire fewer workers. This could apply to care homes or to nursing in an area with one main employer. This argument may not hold if the labour market is closer to competitive, or if the minimum is set above the competitive wage.

A minimum wage may not benefit all low-paid workers because a wage above equilibrium in a competitive market may cut the quantity of labour demanded and raise the quantity supplied, causing unemployment. Workers who lose their jobs, or young and less-skilled workers who find it harder to get a first job, could be worse off. Firms may also cut hours, reduce training or raise prices. The effect on jobs is likely to be smaller where the demand for labour is inelastic or where higher pay raises productivity and lowers turnover.

The minimum wage may also not target the poorest: many low-paid workers live in households that have other income, while the poorest households may have no one in work. Tax credits or training schemes may target poverty better, although they cost the government more.

Overall, a minimum wage is likely to benefit most low-paid workers if it is set at a moderate level, close to the monopsony wage and below the competitive wage, and the evidence from the UK suggests job losses have been small. The benefits could be smaller if it is set too high, in a recession, or in a market with elastic demand for labour. The answer therefore depends on the level, the market structure and the state of the economy.

Top tips

  • Say "derived demand". Demand for labour comes from demand for the product the workers make.
  • Define MRP in full. MPP × MR, and the extra revenue from one more worker. Do not confuse it with MPP.
  • Hire where MRP = wage in a competitive market, and where MCL = MRP for a monopsony.
  • Read the monopsony wage off the supply curve. Go up to MRP, across to MCL, then down to the quantity and up to the supply (ACL) curve.
  • A minimum wage must be above equilibrium to bite in a competitive market. Draw it above, and show the surplus of workers.
  • Monopsony is the exception. A minimum wage between the monopsony and competitive wages can raise employment. Do not forget to say the range.
  • Separate movements from shifts. A wage change moves along the curve; a change in productivity or product demand shifts it.
  • Evaluate with elasticity, size and market structure, and with evidence: the UK's experience suggests small job losses.
  • Economic rent = earnings − transfer earnings. It is largest where supply is most inelastic.
