The 20-Mark Essay

How Paper 4 essays are marked and how to earn the marks for application, analysis and evaluation. Built from the 2023 to 2026 question papers, mark schemes and examiner reports.

The big idea

A 20-mark essay is marked on two scales at once: 14 marks for knowledge, application and analysis (Levels 1 to 3) and 6 marks for evaluation (Levels 1 to 2). Application has no box of its own. It is rewarded inside the 14, and it is what separates an essay that could sit under any question on the topic from one that could only answer this question.

Examiners return to the same three faults year after year: the answer is not applied to the context in the question, the analysis stops after one step, and the evaluation is a list of undeveloped comments.

The paper

2 hours, 60 marks. Section A is a data response question (20). Section B is one micro essay from two (20). Section C is one macro, international or development essay from two (20). That is about 40 minutes per question.

Essays have been marked out of 20 since 2023 (they were 25 before). Questions are no longer split into (a) and (b), so read the whole stem. Use 2023 onwards for practice.

The three skills

Application: using the question's own context, a real example, a figure from the data or a diagram labelled for the situation.

Analysis: a developed chain of cause and effect, not a single step.

Evaluation: a developed, explained judgement on how far, when and for whom the argument holds.

Five rules

  • Answer the question's own words in the opening line of every paragraph.
  • Draw the diagram the question names, label it for the context and use it in your writing.
  • Build three developed chains and apply each one to the context.
  • Evaluate inside each paragraph and again at the end. Explain, do not just name.
  • Finish with a justified judgement that deals with the scope word: always, only, alone, to what extent.

Application is the difference between Level 2 and Level 3

Application means showing you can use economics on this situation, not just on the topic. Level 2 explanations are "limited, over-generalised"; Level 3 are "supported by examples". The examiner reports say the same thing from the other side: the best candidates explain the theory within the context of the question, others fail to apply it.

Four ways to apply

1 · The question's context

Reuse its nouns: the good, the type of country, the event, the policy. A "low-income country" question needs low-income features: reliance on primary exports, low savings, weak institutions, dependence on foreign capital.

S24/42 Q4 was about 2022 supply-shock inflation. Answers that saw cost-push inflation scored well; those that wrote a generic demand-pull essay lost marks.

2 · A real example

A named country, firm, policy or year, with one fact that does work in the argument. The example should change what the analysis says, not decorate it.

Good: the UK's Air Passenger Duty applies per departing passenger, so airlines can pass it on. Weak: "countries like the UK use taxes".

3 · Numbers and data

If the question gives data, use it. If it does not, a simple figure shows control: "a 10% tax and a PED of -0.4 means quantity falls by about 4%".

W24/42 Q4 gave Mexico's data. Nominal wages up 2.8% against inflation of 3.4% means real wages fell. Candidates who wrote about the statistics in the abstract lost the application.

4 · The diagram, in context

Label axes and curves for the situation ("Price of flights ($)", "Number of flights"), put in real-looking values, shade the area that matters, and refer to it by label in the writing.

A perfect diagram that the essay never mentions is a recurring examiner complaint.

The swap testCover the question. Could this paragraph be pasted unchanged under a different question on the same topic? If yes, it is not applied. Add a context noun, an example or a number until it only fits this question.

Analysis is a chain, not a step

The 2023 reports say many answers stopped at "a simple chain of reasoning", which held them at Level 2, and that "brief statements" without developed chains were the main reason few reached Level 3. The June 2026 mark scheme for the average cost question spells out the target: indicative analytical chains and a minimum of three developed points for Level 3.

What a developed chain looks like

Trigger → mechanism → mechanism → effect on the thing the question names → so what for the question

For "a tax on air travel": the tax raises the price of a flight → quantity demanded falls along the demand curve, by more if demand is elastic → fewer flights → less carbon and noise → output moves towards the level where marginal social benefit equals marginal cost → allocative efficiency improves. That is six links. "A tax reduces demand and so pollution" is two.

The chain testJoin every link with "because", "so" or "which means". If you can delete a link and the sentence still reads naturally, you have not explained it. If a link needs the examiner to guess, add the missing step.

Evaluation is 6 marks, and it questions the statement

Evaluation is expected even when the question does not say "evaluate". It is not more analysis: in M23 Q4 the report says analysing the impact of expenditure-reducing policies "did not constitute" evaluation, which meant questioning how true it is that they cause significant unemployment. To reach E2, a comment must include explanation based on economic concepts. Naming a factor earns E1; explaining how it changes the outcome earns E2.

S24/42 Q2: candidates who said the policy effect "depended on price elasticity" were placed in E1. Those who explained how elasticity changed the outcome were placed in E2.

The formula

Lever → because → so what for the answer

E1: "However, this depends on the price elasticity of demand."
E2: "However, if business travellers have few substitutes, demand for flights may be inelastic, so the tax could raise the price without cutting flights much. The externality would then remain largely uncorrected, and the policy would look better as a revenue raiser than as a way to reach the social optimum."

Where evaluation goes

1 · End of every analysis paragraph

One or two sentences on that paragraph's point only: "This may not hold if ...". It stops the evaluation piling up at the end and shows judgement throughout.

2 · A weighing paragraph

One short paragraph that compares the strengths of your arguments, or two policies, or the short and long run. Evaluation on the "relative" part of a question lives here.

3 · The judgement

A conclusion that answers the question using its scope word. A summary of what you already wrote earns little. A verdict with a reason and a condition earns the top of E2.

Beat the scope word

Strong words in the question are there to be tested. Match each to the move that evaluates it. Examples from 2023 to 2026: "will always benefit the producer" (S23 Q3), "automatically improve living standards" (M24 Q5), "gains all the benefits ... avoiding all the costs" (S25 Q5), "equally beneficial" (S25/42 Q5), "alone" (S25/44 Q4).

Writing the judgement

Verdict on the statement + because (your strongest reason) + it depends on (condition) + what you would change in the short or long run

Example for "Larger firms always have lower average costs" (S26 Q2): "Larger firms often achieve lower average costs, because economies of scale such as technical and purchasing economies are likely to dominate up to the minimum efficient scale. They do not always, since diseconomies of scale, low-MES industries and digital technology can let smaller firms match them. The statement is therefore too strong; the result depends on how the firm's size compares with the MES in its industry."

Spend the first five minutes on the question

Examiners keep reporting the same failure: not reading the question carefully before "putting pen to paper". Most of the wording is there for a reason. The command word tells you what to do, the limit word tells you what to test, the context tells you where to apply, and the diagram demand tells you what to draw.

Decoding checklist

  • Command word. Evaluate, assess, consider or discuss? Each ends in a judgement.
  • Limit word. Always, only, alone, all, equally, automatically, fully, "to what extent". Plan how you will test it.
  • Parts. Two policies means two. Both income groups means both. Productivity and wages and employment means all three.
  • Context. Low-income country, 2022 inflation, a merger in one industry, a table of Mexican data. Write the nouns on your plan.
  • Diagram. "With the help of a diagram" and "injections and withdrawals graph" are marked requirements. Draw it even when not named if it supports analysis, as with price discrimination or AD/AS.
  • Direction. A fall in interest rates is not a rise. Expenditure-switching is not expenditure-reducing. A negative output gap is not a positive one.
  • Branch. Micro, macro or international? Keep the whole answer in it.

One paragraph, three levels

The same point in an essay on M23 Q2: assess the extent to which a government can intervene to correct the market failure caused by negative externalities from air travel. The sums in brackets are rough, illustrative marks for this paragraph's contribution to the 14.

Level 1 · about 3 of 14

Air travel causes pollution. The government can put a tax on flights. This makes tickets more expensive so people fly less and there is less pollution, which is good for everyone.

Descriptive. No economic terms, no mechanism, no diagram, nothing specific to flights.

Level 2 · about 8 of 14

Air travel creates a negative externality because marginal social benefit is below marginal private benefit. A government could use an indirect tax, which reduces demand. Quantity falls towards the social optimum so allocative efficiency improves. The tax may be hard to set at the right level, so the government might only partly correct the market failure.

Correct and analytical, but it could be pasted under an essay on cigarettes or plastic bags. No context, no example, no diagram reference.

Level 3 · about 13 of 14, with evaluation

A government can intervene to correct this market failure by taxing air travel. Passengers compare only their private benefit with the price of a ticket, so flights are bought up to Qm, where MPB equals marginal cost. The emissions and noise that fall on people who never bought a ticket mean MSB lies below MPB, so Qm exceeds the efficient quantity Q* and the shaded triangle shows the welfare loss. A tax per departing passenger, like the UK's Air Passenger Duty, raises the price of a ticket, and if it equals the external cost it shifts MPB down to MSB, so quantity demanded may fall from Qm to Q*. Fewer flights means less carbon and noise, which removes the welfare loss and moves the market to allocative efficiency. However, no government can measure the external cost of a flight exactly. A tax set too low leaves part of the welfare loss, and one set too high pushes flights below Q*. Where business travellers have few substitutes, demand may be inelastic and the tax could cut flights only a little. A tax can therefore move the market towards allocative efficiency, which supports the view that a government can correct much of the failure, but not exactly.

What changed from Level 2: the diagram is used by label (Qm, Q*, shaded triangle), the chain has more links, there is a real policy and a real type of traveller, and the evaluation explains how elasticity and measurement change the result before linking back to the question.

Model answers

Essay 1 Assess 20 marks The use of air travel leads to market failure caused by negative externalities. With the help of a diagram, assess the extent to which a government can intervene to correct this market failure. (M23 Q2)
RemindersDiagram demanded: no correct diagram, no Level 3. Air travel is a consumption externality, so draw MPB, MSB and MC. Examiners marked production diagrams down. "The extent" means you finish with a degree: considerable but incomplete, not yes or no. Two or three policies, each analysed and evaluated.

Market failure occurs when the price mechanism allocates resources inefficiently. For air travel it is allocative inefficiency: a negative externality of consumption is a cost imposed on third parties, so marginal social benefit (MSB) is below marginal private benefit (MPB), and allocative efficiency requires output where MSB equals marginal cost (MC). A government can correct this market failure to a considerable extent through taxation and regulation, but a full correction is unlikely because of measurement problems, time lags and cost.

The market produces where MPB equals MC, at Qm flights, because passengers and airlines ignore the emissions and noise that fall on others. Residents under the flight paths at airports such as Heathrow, and everyone exposed to the climate effects of aviation emissions, bear costs they did not choose. MSB therefore lies below MPB, the efficient output is Q*, and the shaded triangle between Q* and Qm is the welfare loss from over-consumption.

A government can intervene to correct this market failure by taxing air travel. A tax per departing passenger, like the UK's Air Passenger Duty, raises the price of a ticket. If the tax equals the external cost, MPB shifts down to MSB, quantity demanded falls from Qm to Q*, the cost is internalised and the welfare loss is removed. Short-haul routes such as London to Paris have close substitutes in rail, so demand may be elastic and the tax could cut flights noticeably. However, the external cost of a flight is very hard to measure. A tax set too low leaves part of the welfare loss, and one set too high takes flights below Q*. Where business travellers have few substitutes, demand may be inelastic and the tax could raise revenue with little effect on flights. So a tax can move the market towards allocative efficiency, which supports the view that a government can correct much of the failure, though not exactly.

A government can also intervene to correct this market failure by limiting emissions directly. The EU Emissions Trading System, which covers flights within Europe, caps total emissions and lets airlines buy and trade permits. The cap restricts the quantity of emissions, so flights are likely to fall, and because airlines that cut emissions cheaply can sell permits to those that cannot, the cap could be met at lower cost than a flat rule. The price of permits raises the cost of flying much like a tax. However, the cap has to be set accurately and enforced across many airlines. Flights are international, so passengers could switch to airports outside the scheme, which weakens the correction.

A government could also try negative advertising to shift demand to the left by changing how travellers value flying. Campaigns linked to "flight shame" in Sweden suggest attitudes can change. But campaigns are costly, work slowly and may not persuade enough travellers, so the effect is uncertain and probably smaller than a change in price. A complete ban would be precise, but it removes consumer choice and jobs.

Overall, a government can correct the market failure from air travel to a considerable but incomplete extent. A well-calibrated tax and a permits scheme both change incentives, so they are likely to be more reliable than persuasion. But the external cost is hard to measure, flights cross borders so action by one country can leak, and policies take years to work through airline fleets and habits. The extent is therefore likely to be greatest in the long run and where policies are combined, and it depends on how elastic demand is on each route.

How this scoresLikely top of Level 3 for knowledge, application and analysis: a correct consumption-externality diagram used in the text, three policies each applied, chains of five or more links. Likely 5 to 6 for evaluation: explained points at the end of every paragraph and a judgement that answers "the extent". A weaker answer draws a production externality, lists tax, ban and subsidy in a line each, and evaluates with "it is expensive".
Essay 2 Assess 20 marks Some firms frequently use price discrimination. Assess the view that when this occurs, price discrimination will always benefit the producer at the expense of the consumer and society. (S23 Q3)
Reminders"Always" and "at the expense of" are the targets. The statement names three parties (producer, consumer, society), so each needs a verdict. No diagram is demanded, but the examiner report points to the third-degree price discrimination diagram as the way into Level 3.
Diagram to draw. Two side-by-side panels. Market A: steeper (less elastic) demand and its MR. Market B: flatter (more elastic) demand and MR. The same MC in both. In each panel read output where MR = MC and take the price up to demand, so P(A) is greater than P(B). Shade the consumer surplus lost in A and kept in B.

Price discrimination is charging different prices to different consumers for the same product where the differences are not explained by differences in cost. It requires market power, markets that can be separated with different price elasticities of demand, and no resale. In third-degree price discrimination the firm splits consumers into groups, such as peak and off-peak travellers, and charges the higher price to the group with the less elastic demand. It is likely to benefit the producer, but "always" and "at the expense of the consumer and society" are too strong, because some consumers and society may gain.

Price discrimination is likely to benefit the producer in most cases because it converts consumer surplus into revenue. In each market the firm sets MR equal to MC, which gives a higher price where demand is less elastic and a lower price where it is more elastic. Revenue and profit exceed those from a single price because each group pays closer to its willingness to pay. Airlines charge more for last-minute business bookings than for early leisure bookings on the same flight, and rail operators charge more at peak times, so the same seat yields more revenue. However, the producer does not always gain. Separating customers costs money for ticketing systems and restrictions, and if consumers can resell or avoid the higher price, profit may rise little or even fall. A group that feels unfairly treated could switch to rivals. The producer is therefore very likely to gain, but not in every case.

The view that price discrimination is at the expense of the consumer is only partly true. Consumers in the market with less elastic demand pay more than under a single price and lose consumer surplus. Consumers in the elastic market pay less, so more of them can buy, and some who would be priced out entirely gain access and some surplus. Student and senior discounts at cinemas and museums may let people visit who otherwise would not. The net effect on consumers depends on how large each group is and on how much extra output the lower price brings, so consumers as a whole could gain or lose.

Price discrimination may benefit society as well as the producer where it increases output and funds investment. If the firm would otherwise charge one high monopoly price, selling to the low-price group raises output towards the allocatively efficient level, and higher profits can be reinvested in research. That supports dynamic efficiency and could lower costs and prices in future. Some pharmaceutical firms charge lower prices for medicines in low-income countries than in high-income ones and may use the high-income revenue to fund research. However, society could lose if the practice reinforces market power, if the higher price falls on a group with no alternative, such as patients with a rare condition, or if the extra profit is not reinvested. Who ought to pay more is also a value judgement.

Overall, price discrimination is likely to raise producer profit when the firm can separate markets, but the claim that it will always benefit the producer at the expense of consumers and society is overstated. The result depends on whether the firm can prevent resale, how different the elasticities are, whether output expands in the lower-priced market and whether profits are reinvested. Where it widens access, as with student fares, society could benefit; where it exploits a captive group, consumers and society are more likely to lose.

How this scoresLikely Level 3 for the 14: the conditions are stated, the MR = MC logic is explained for each market, and there is an application in every paragraph. Likely E2: each paragraph questions "always" with an explained reason, and the judgement says what the outcome depends on. A Level 2 answer would say price discrimination is "unfair" and "good for firms" without separating the three parties.
Essay 3 Evaluate 20 marks In 2022, many countries experienced a high rate of inflation caused by disruptions to the supply of goods and services arising from the Covid-19 pandemic and the conflict between Russia and Ukraine. In one country, the government cut taxes and the central bank raised interest rates. Evaluate the likely impact of these policies on that government's ability to control inflation. (S24/42 Q4)
RemindersContext is the question. The cause is a supply shock, so it is cost-push inflation, not demand-pull. The examiner report says candidates who failed to recognise this limited their marks. Two policies, so analyse both. Use an AD/AS diagram (macro labels, not D and S). "Likely impact" invites hedged, qualified analysis.

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. Here it is cost-push inflation: disruption to supply during the Covid-19 pandemic and after the conflict between Russia and Ukraine raised the cost of energy, food and shipping, which shifts short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) to the left. A tax cut is expansionary fiscal policy and a rise in the central bank's interest rate is contractionary monetary policy; both work on aggregate demand (AD). These policies are likely to give the government limited control over inflation, because they target AD while the cause lies in supply, and they pull AD in opposite directions.

The likely impact of these policies has to start from the cause of the inflation. Higher energy and shipping costs raise firms' costs at every level of output, so SRAS shifts left, the price level rises and real GDP falls below potential. That is a negative output gap with inflation, so demand-side policies are unlikely to remove the cause. In 2022 many European economies faced sharp rises in gas and electricity prices after gas supplies from Russia were reduced, which raised costs across manufacturing and food production.

The tax cut is likely to reduce the government's ability to control inflation. Lower income tax raises disposable income and consumption, and lower corporation tax could raise investment, so AD shifts right. With output below potential the extra demand raises real GDP a little, but the price level rises further, adding demand-pull pressure to cost-push inflation. The UK's September 2022 mini-budget announced large tax cuts when inflation was close to 10%. The pound fell and government borrowing costs rose, which suggests markets doubted that the policy fitted with controlling inflation. However, the effect depends on how much of the extra income households spend rather than save and how much leaks into imports. A cut targeted at low-income households, who are hit hardest by energy prices, could support spending without a large rise in demand, so size and design matter.

The rise in interest rates is likely to help control inflation, but at a cost to output. Higher rates raise the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving, so consumption and investment fall and AD shifts left. They may also attract hot money, raising the exchange rate and lowering the price of imported energy and food, which directly eases cost-push pressure. Weaker demand reduces pressure on wages and could help to anchor inflation expectations. The Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 0.1% in late 2021 to 5.25% by August 2023, while UK inflation peaked above 11% in October 2022. However, because the problem is on the supply side, lower AD is likely to cut output and raise unemployment further below potential. The effect is slow because of time lags of a year or more, particularly where households have fixed-rate mortgages, and interest rates are a blunt tool against imported inflation.

The two policies are likely to work against each other. The tax cut shifts AD right while the rate rise shifts AD left, so the net shift may be small, and neither shifts SRAS back to the right. The price level then falls mainly as the supply disruption fades. A more effective approach may be supply-side policy, such as diversifying energy supply, or temporary support for the most affected households that does not add to general demand. These work more slowly and cost money, so there is a trade-off between speed and effectiveness.

Overall, the policies are likely to limit rather than strengthen the government's ability to control inflation. The rate rise is likely to help by reducing AD and import costs, but it costs output, while the tax cut is likely to work against it. Because the source of inflation is a supply shock, the extent of control probably depends on how quickly supply recovers, on how large and well targeted the tax cut is, and on whether inflation expectations stay anchored. In the short run control is likely to be weak; in the long run recovery of supply is likely to matter more than either policy.

How this scoresLikely Level 3 for the 14 through accurate AD/AS analysis of both policies applied to a supply-shock context. Likely E2 because the evaluation explains time lags, leakages and design, compares the policies, and the judgement addresses "ability to control". A weaker answer treats the inflation as demand-pull, explains the two policies in the abstract, and concludes "it depends".
Essay 4 Evaluate 20 marks Evaluate the likely impact of globalisation on a low-income country's standard of living. (S24/42 Q5)
RemindersThe question has three parts that examiners expected to see joined up: globalisation, a low-income country and the standard of living. Weaker answers dropped the standard of living or the income level. Define the standard of living (material and non-material) and use it in the evaluation. A PPC or LRAS diagram of potential growth would support the analysis even though it is not demanded.

Globalisation is the growing integration of economies through trade, capital flows, the movement of people and the transfer of technology. A low-income country (LIC) typically has low GNI per head, relies on primary exports and has limited capital and skills. The standard of living has a material side, such as real GNI per head and access to goods, and a non-material side, such as health, education and working conditions. Globalisation is likely to raise the average standard of living in an LIC through investment and trade, but the gains may be shared unevenly and come with costs, so the overall impact depends on how the benefits are used.

Globalisation is likely to raise the standard of living of a low-income country through foreign investment and trade. Foreign direct investment by multinational corporations is an injection into the circular flow, and with a multiplier it raises incomes and employment. The new capital, technology and training also raise productive capacity, shifting the PPC or LRAS outwards, so potential growth rises. Higher incomes raise tax revenue that the government could spend on health and education, which raise non-material living standards. Vietnam's export-led growth since its Doi Moi reforms in 1986, including electronics production by foreign firms such as Samsung in northern Vietnam, helped raise average incomes substantially, although Vietnam is now classed as lower-middle income. However, the gains depend on local linkages. If foreign firms import most inputs and send profits abroad, for example through transfer pricing, the local gain may be limited to low-skilled jobs.

Globalisation could also reduce the standard of living in a low-income country where it brings exploitation and negative externalities. Multinationals may locate where wages and regulations are weakest, so workers could face low pay and unsafe conditions. Production may impose pollution on local communities, a negative externality that GNI per head does not count. Local firms may be outcompeted by cheaper imports, raising unemployment, and reliance on one export leaves the country exposed to price falls. In Bangladesh the garment industry employs millions of people, mostly women, and has raised household incomes, but the collapse of the Rana Plaza factory building in 2013, which killed more than 1,100 workers, showed the cost of weak safety enforcement. The extent of these costs depends on how well governments enforce rules, so the same globalisation can have different effects in countries with different institutions.

The likely impact on the standard of living also depends on who gains. If the gains go to a small group, such as owners of export firms or workers near a few industrial zones, average income may rise while most households see little change, and a rising Gini coefficient could signal that inequality is widening. Real GNI per head also misses non-material factors, so a country can grow richer on paper while health and working conditions stagnate. For that reason an indicator such as the multidimensional poverty index, which includes health, education and living standards, may give a fairer view of the impact than GNI per head alone.

Overall, globalisation is likely to raise the standard of living in a low-income country over time, mainly through investment, jobs and technology transfer, but the size and distribution of the gain are uncertain. The impact is likely to be strongest where the government enforces labour and environmental standards, invests tax revenue in health and education, and builds links between foreign firms and local suppliers. Where these conditions are missing, the gains could be narrow and the costs, from exploitation to pollution, could outweigh them for many households. A judgement therefore needs to be tied to the country's institutions and to the measure of the standard of living used.

How this scoresLikely Level 3 for the 14 because the answer joins globalisation, low-income features and the standard of living, with two real examples and developed chains. Likely E2 because the evaluation explains why gains depend on linkages, enforcement and distribution, and the measure of the standard of living is itself evaluated. A weaker answer lists "jobs, pollution, exploitation" in bullets and never mentions the standard of living.

Top tips

  • Choose by application, not by favourite topic. In the first minute, jot two examples or context points you could use for each option. Pick the question you can apply best.
  • Open every analysis paragraph with the question's words and close it by tying back to what was asked.
  • Draw the named diagram first, large, and label it for the context. Refer to Qm, Q*, AD₂ or the shaded area in your writing.
  • Aim for three developed chains of four or more links, each with a real example, number or context point.
  • Define precisely, once. Put all the definitions in one opening paragraph, with your line on the question.
  • Evaluate at the end of every paragraph with lever, because, so what. Then compare and judge at the end.
  • Use qualified language for effects and predictions: could, may, is likely to, tends to. Keep definitions and calculations exact. Where the question uses a strong word, argue on its terms and test it.
  • Do what the number says. Two policies means two. Both income groups means both. A "relative" question needs comparison, not two descriptions.
  • Stay in the branch and the model. Micro question, micro answer. Indifference curves question, no marginal utility.
  • Finish the judgement. It should answer the scope word, name the strongest reason and say what it depends on.
  • Keep it relevant and concise. Examiners reward a short, directed answer in full. Writing everything you know does not earn more.
  • Keep a bank. Two or three examples each for externalities, market structures, inflation policy, trade policy and globalisation in low-income countries.
