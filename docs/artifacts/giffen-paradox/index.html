The Giffen Paradox

A Giffen good is bought more when its price rises and less when its price falls: the one case where a demand curve slopes the wrong way. Indifference curves and budget lines show why.

The big idea

Normally a lower price means a higher quantity demanded. A Giffen good is the exception: it is so strongly inferior, and takes up so much of a poor household's budget, that when its price falls the consumer feels richer, switches towards better goods and ends up buying less of it. Its demand curve slopes upwards.

The tools: budget line and indifference curve

Budget line

Shows every combination of two goods the consumer can afford with a given income and prices. It is a straight line from M ÷ PY on the Y axis to M ÷ PX on the X axis.

Slope = −PX ÷ PY

A fall in the price of X pivots the line outwards around the Y-axis intercept. A change in income shifts it in parallel.

Indifference curve

Shows every combination of the two goods that gives the consumer the same satisfaction. Curves are convex to the origin because of the diminishing marginal rate of substitution. Curves further from the origin mean higher satisfaction.

The consumer maximises utility where the budget line is tangent to the highest reachable indifference curve.

Splitting a price change into two effects

When the price of X changes, the consumer's choice moves from the old tangency A to a new one F. We split the move into two steps using a hypothetical budget line that is parallel to the new budget line but tangent to the original indifference curve.

Substitution effect (A to S)

The change in relative prices makes X cheaper compared with Y, so the consumer switches towards X, staying on the same indifference curve. It always points the "normal" way: a lower price raises quantity demanded.

Income effect (S to F)

The price fall also raises real income, moving the consumer to a different indifference curve. For a normal good this raises demand for X. For an inferior good it reduces demand for X, because the consumer can afford better alternatives.

Normal, inferior and Giffen goods compared

Effects of a fall in the price of X (reverse the signs for a rise):

Key ruleAll Giffen goods are inferior, but most inferior goods are not Giffen. The income effect must be bigger than the substitution effect and work in the opposite direction.

When might a Giffen good appear?

  • Strongly inferior: the income effect has to be large and negative.
  • A big share of the budget: the price change has to change real income a lot, so the good is typically a staple for a very poor household.
  • No close substitutes: otherwise the substitution effect would be large and would win.

Why the curves stay bowed toward the origin

Both indifference curves are drawn convex to the origin: the consumer needs ever more of one good to give up a unit of the other as it becomes scarce. This diminishing marginal rate of substitution keeps the tangency well defined. It is never relaxed to make the Giffen result "work".

Where the paradox actually comes from

The convex curves guarantee the substitution effect always points the normal way. A Giffen result needs nothing exotic in the shape of the curve. It needs X to be so strongly inferior that the income effect outweighs the substitution effect and reverses the sign of the total.

6 · Multiple choice

Giffen goods are rare and the evidence is contested. Treat each example as an illustration, and hedge in an exam.

Staple foods for very poor households

The standard textbook case is a basic staple such as rice, bread or potatoes that takes up most of a very poor household's budget. If its price rises, the household may have to cut back on meat or vegetables and buy even more of the staple to stay fed. Some researchers have looked for this behaviour in field studies, and they found it only in particular circumstances.

Use it for: showing why the income effect can be large and why Giffen behaviour needs a big budget share and no close substitutes.

The Irish potato famine

Textbooks often cite potatoes in nineteenth-century Ireland as a possible Giffen good. Many economists dispute this, because the shortage may have reduced supply rather than moved demand along a rising curve.

Use it for: an example with a caution. Say that the evidence is debated.

Instant noodles and own-brand basics

When incomes rise, some households buy fewer instant noodles or own-brand products and switch to better goods. These are inferior goods with a negative income effect. A price fall does not usually reduce quantity demanded, so they are not Giffen goods.

Use it for: showing the difference between an inferior good and a Giffen good.

Luxury brands and status goods

Demand for some designer goods may rise when the price rises, because a high price signals exclusivity. This is not a Giffen effect: it comes from status, not from an income effect on a poor household.

Use it for: avoiding a common mistake. Say that such goods are sometimes called Veblen goods.

Why economists care

Giffen goods show that the law of demand has a logical exception, and that splitting a price change into substitution and income effects explains why. It is also a reminder that a price rise hits the poor hardest when they spend a lot of their income on one item.

Use it for: evaluation: the exception is rare, so the downward-sloping demand curve remains a good general rule.

Q2 Calculate 4 marks A consumer has an income of $120. The price of X is $3 and the price of Y is $2. Calculate the maximum quantity of each good, and the slope of the budget line. State what happens to the budget line if the price of X rises to $6.
ReminderDivide income by each price. The slope is the price ratio. A price change pivots the line around the intercept of the unchanged good.
Maximum X = M ÷ PX = 120 ÷ 3 = 40 units. Maximum Y = 120 ÷ 2 = 60 units. The slope is PX ÷ PY = 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5. If the price of X rises to $6, the maximum of X falls to 20 units while the maximum of Y stays at 60, so the budget line pivots inwards around the Y-axis intercept and becomes steeper.
Common mistakeShifting the whole line in parallel. Only an income change shifts it in parallel.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain, using a diagram, the difference between the substitution effect and the income effect of a fall in the price of a good.
ReminderName the points on your diagram (A, S, F). Say which indifference curve each move is along or between.

When the price of X falls, the budget line pivots outwards and the consumer moves from the original equilibrium A to a new equilibrium F. We split this into two steps using a hypothetical budget line parallel to the new one and tangent to the original indifference curve at S.

The substitution effect is the move from A to S along the same indifference curve. X is now relatively cheaper, so the consumer switches towards X, and this effect always raises quantity demanded. The income effect is the move from S to F, onto a higher indifference curve. The lower price raises real income, so the consumer is likely to buy more of a normal good but less of an inferior good.

Common mistakeSaying the income effect is always positive. It depends on whether the good is normal or inferior.
Q4 Explain 8 marks Explain why the demand curve for a Giffen good slopes upward.
ReminderStart with the two effects of a price change, state their directions for an inferior good, then say which is bigger for a Giffen good.

A Giffen good is a strongly inferior good, so a price change has a substitution effect and an income effect that work in opposite directions. If the price of the good rises, the substitution effect is likely to reduce the quantity demanded because the good is now relatively dearer, and the consumer switches to other goods.

The rise in price also cuts the consumer's real income. Because the good is inferior, lower real income increases demand for it, so the income effect raises the quantity demanded. For a Giffen good the income effect is larger than the substitution effect, so the net result is that quantity demanded rises when price rises, and the demand curve slopes upward. This could happen for a staple food that takes up most of a very poor household's budget: when its price rises, the household may be unable to afford meat or vegetables and buys more of the staple.

This needs a large budget share and no close substitutes, so Giffen goods are rare, and the evidence for real examples is limited.

Common mistakeDescribing only the final result. The marks are for the two opposing effects and which one is larger.
Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss whether a rise in the price of a good could lead to an increase in the quantity demanded.
ReminderOpen each paragraph with the question's wording. Finish each paragraph with a short evaluative point and a link back to the question. Finish with a judgement.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

The law of demand says that a rise in price normally reduces quantity demanded. A price change has a substitution effect, along an indifference curve, and an income effect, caused by the change in real income. For most goods both effects work in the same direction, or the substitution effect dominates.

A rise in the price of a good could lead to an increase in the quantity demanded if it is a Giffen good. For a strongly inferior good, the price rise reduces real income, which increases demand for the good, and if this income effect outweighs the substitution effect the net result is a rise in quantity demanded. A basic staple food for a very poor household could behave in this way. However, it requires a high share of income, no close substitutes and a very strong income effect, so real examples may be rare. A Giffen good is therefore one case where a price rise could increase quantity demanded.

A rise in the price of a good could also be followed by an increase in quantity demanded if the price rise signals higher quality or status, or if consumers expect further rises. Buyers may speed up purchases before the price goes up again, or see a high price as a sign of exclusivity. Some luxury fashion brands may benefit from this. These are not Giffen effects and are not caused by income effects, and the effect may not last if expectations change.

Overall, a rise in price could raise the quantity demanded in a few cases, but it is unlikely for most goods, because the substitution effect usually dominates and the income effect for normal goods works in the same direction. The downward-sloping demand curve remains a good general rule, and the exceptions depend on how poor the consumers are and on the alternatives available.

Common mistakeTreating luxury or status goods as Giffen goods. They are different explanations.

Top tips

  • Always name the two effects. Any answer about a Giffen good needs the substitution effect and the income effect.
  • The substitution effect is always "normal". A price fall raises quantity, a price rise lowers it. It is the income effect that can reverse the total.
  • All Giffen goods are inferior, but not all inferior goods are Giffen. The income effect has to be bigger than the substitution effect.
  • Label A, S and F on the diagram and the hypothetical budget line. Say that S lies on the original indifference curve.
  • Budget line pivots, it does not shift, when one price changes. It shifts in parallel when income changes.
  • Do not confuse Giffen goods with Veblen goods. Veblen goods are bought for status, not because of an income effect.
  • Hedge the real-world claims. Say that Giffen goods are rare and the evidence is debated.
