The Giffen Paradox

A Giffen good is bought more when its price rises, and less when its price falls — the one case where demand curves slope the wrong way. The diagram below decomposes the price change into a substitution effect and an income effect to show why.

Why the curves stay bowed toward the origin

Both indifference curves are drawn as true convex-to-origin curves (rectangular hyperbolas, y = k⁄x): the consumer needs ever more of one good to give up a unit of the other as that good becomes scarce. This diminishing marginal rate of substitution is what keeps the tangency solution well-defined — it's never relaxed to make the Giffen result "work."

Where the paradox actually comes from

The convex curves guarantee the substitution effect always points the "normal" way — more X when X gets relatively cheaper, less when it gets dearer. A Giffen result needs nothing exotic in the curve's shape; it needs X to be so strongly inferior that the income effect of the price change outweighs that substitution effect and reverses its sign in the total.
