Multipliers, Markets and Monopsony

Three topics built one move at a time: the multiplier and accelerator, imperfect labour markets, and the effect of a trade union or minimum wage on a monopsony.

Three big ideas

1. A change in spending is multiplied: the final change in national income is bigger than the initial injection. 2. Real labour markets are imperfect, so wages do not always equal the marginal revenue product of labour. 3. When one employer dominates the market (a monopsony), a minimum wage or union can raise pay and jobs.

1 · The multiplier and the accelerator

An injection (government spending, investment or exports) becomes someone's income. They spend part of it, which becomes someone else's income, and so on. The rounds shrink because each time a share leaks out as saving, tax or imports.

k = ΔY ÷ ΔJ = 1 ÷ MPS = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC)
Open economy with tax: k = 1 ÷ (MPS + MRT + MPM)

Worked example. MPS = 0.2, MRT = 0.2 and MPM = 0.1. The marginal propensity to withdraw is 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.5, so k = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2. A £30m rise in government spending raises national income by 2 × £30m = £60m.

What limits the multiplier?

  • Large leakages: high saving, tax and import rates.
  • No spare capacity: extra spending raises prices instead of output.
  • Time lags: the rounds take months or years.
  • Crowding out and expectations may offset the stimulus.

The accelerator

I = v × ΔY

Net investment depends on the change in income, where v is the capital-output ratio. If income growth slows, investment can fall while income is still rising, which helps explain booms and busts.

2 · Imperfect labour markets

In a perfect labour market the wage equals the marginal revenue product (MRP) of the last worker hired. Real markets depart from this in several ways:

ImperfectionEffect on wages and jobs
Monopsony: one dominant employerWage and employment below the competitive level
Trade union: workers act togetherWage pushed above the competitive level
Occupational and geographical immobilityWage gaps persist; shortages and surpluses of workers
Imperfect informationWorkers may accept less; poor job matching
Minimum wageA legal floor: jobs may fall or rise, depending on the market
Non-wage factors, discriminationPay gaps unrelated to productivity

Wage differentials

Surgeons earn more than cleaners because the demand for them (their MRP) is higher and the supply is smaller, because training takes years. High demand plus low supply gives a high wage.

Transfer earnings and economic rent

Transfer earnings are what a worker must earn to stay in the current job. Economic rent is anything above that. The less elastic the supply of labour, the greater the economic rent.

3 · Monopsony, unions and the minimum wage

A monopsony faces the whole upward-sloping supply of labour, so hiring one more worker means paying every worker more. Its marginal cost of labour (MC) lies above the supply curve (AC). It hires where MC = MRP and pays the wage on the supply curve.

The diagrams in the Watch tab use S: W = 10 + 0.5Q, MC = 10 + Q and MRP = 100 − 0.5Q. They give the following outcomes.

Key ruleWhether a minimum wage costs jobs depends on how much monopsony power employers have and where the wage is set. In a monopsony it can raise employment up to the competitive wage; above that, jobs fall.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms so they stay true. Look up current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

Infrastructure spending after a recession

Governments sometimes respond to a downturn with spending on roads, rail or housebuilding. Construction workers spend their wages in local shops, which pay their own staff, so the first round is followed by many more. The total effect is likely to be larger when there is spare capacity and smaller if a lot of the spending goes on imports or is saved.

Use it for: the multiplier, leakages, and spare capacity as an evaluation point.

A small, open economy

In an economy that imports a high share of what it consumes, much of any extra income is spent abroad. The marginal propensity to import is high, so the multiplier is likely to be small. Larger, less open economies tend to have bigger multipliers.

Use it for: MPM as a leakage and why a stimulus may have less effect in some countries.

The car and housebuilding industries

Firms making cars, machinery or houses may order many new machines and sites when demand is growing fast. If demand growth slows, even while sales are still rising, they may cut orders sharply. Investment goods industries often swing more than the economy as a whole.

Use it for: the accelerator, and why investment is volatile.

Care homes and local labour markets

In some areas one or two large employers hire most care workers or nurses, and workers cannot easily move. These employers may be able to pay less than the value of what workers produce. A minimum wage may then raise pay without costing jobs.

Use it for: monopsony power and evaluating the effect of a minimum wage.

Surgeons, cleaners and footballers

Surgeons earn more than cleaners because demand for them is high and supply is limited by training. Top footballers earn far more than they would in their next best job, which means much of their pay may be economic rent. A cleaner's pay may be much closer to transfer earnings.

Use it for: wage differentials, transfer earnings and economic rent.

Trade unions and public sector pay

Unions negotiating for teachers, nurses or rail workers can push pay above the level the market alone might set. If the employer is the main buyer of that labour, the outcome may be higher pay with little loss of jobs; in a competitive market the same pay rise might cost jobs.

Use it for: the union and monopsony diagrams, and bilateral monopoly.

Q1 Define 2 marks Define the multiplier.
ReminderTwo marks means two ideas: what it compares, and the direction (bigger final change).
The multiplier is the ratio of the final change in national income to the initial change in injections (or autonomous spending), so the final rise in income is a multiple of the initial injection.
Common mistakeWriting "the multiplier makes income rise more". Define it as a ratio.
Q2 Calculate 4 marks In an economy the MPS is 0.2, the marginal rate of tax is 0.2 and the marginal propensity to import is 0.1. Calculate the multiplier and the rise in national income from a £30m rise in government spending.
ReminderAdd all three leakages first. Then k = 1 ÷ MPW.
Marginal propensity to withdraw = MPS + MRT + MPM = 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.5. Multiplier k = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2. Rise in national income = k × ΔJ = 2 × £30m = £60m. The rise is twice the initial injection.
Common mistakeUsing only the MPS: 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. In an open economy with taxes all three leakages count.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain how a trade union could raise both wages and employment in a monopsony.
ReminderDescribe the monopsony outcome first (MC = MRP, wage on the supply curve), then what the union wage does to MC.

A monopsony is a single dominant buyer of labour. It faces the whole upward-sloping supply curve, so to hire more workers it must raise the wage for all of them, and its marginal cost of labour lies above the supply curve. It hires where MC = MRP, which gives fewer jobs and a wage below MRP.

If a union negotiates a wage above the monopsony wage but at or below the competitive wage, the wage becomes fixed over that range of employment, so the marginal cost of an extra worker is just the agreed wage. The firm can hire more workers without raising the wage for existing ones, so profit-maximising employment is likely to rise. This could happen for care workers where one employer dominates the local market. If the union pushes the wage above the competitive level, employment is likely to fall again.

Common mistakeSaying a union always raises unemployment. That is only true in a competitive market, or above the competitive wage.
Q4 Explain 8 marks Explain how the accelerator may cause investment to fall even while national income is still rising.
ReminderStart with the formula, use numbers if you can, and say why firms respond to the rate of change.

The accelerator theory states that net investment depends on the rate of change of national income, I = v × ΔY, where v is the capital-output ratio. Firms invest to add capacity so they can produce more, and the amount of new capital they need depends on how much extra output they expect to produce.

Suppose v = 2 and income rises by £15bn in one year, so investment is £30bn. If income rises by only £10bn the next year, investment falls to £20bn, even though income is still rising. Firms see spare capacity opening up, so they order fewer new machines. Housebuilders and car makers could respond in this way when growth slows.

The model assumes firms have no spare capacity and a stable capital-output ratio, and firms may not react so mechanically, because they look at expected demand and the cost of borrowing as well. The accelerator therefore helps explain why investment is volatile.

Common mistakeSaying investment depends on the level of income. It depends on the change.
Q5 Discuss 12 marks Discuss whether a national minimum wage is likely to reduce employment.
ReminderOpen each paragraph with the question's wording. Finish each paragraph with a short evaluative point and a link back to the question. Finish with a judgement.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A national minimum wage is a legal wage floor. It only affects the market if it is set above the equilibrium wage. Employment is the number of workers hired at the wage.

A national minimum wage is likely to reduce employment in a competitive labour market. If the floor is set above the equilibrium wage, employers move up their MRP curve and demand fewer workers, while more people want to work at the higher wage, so there is unemployment. Teenagers in unskilled jobs could be hit hardest because their MRP is low. The size of the job loss depends on the elasticity of demand for labour, which may be low if the wage is only a small share of firms' costs. In a competitive market, therefore, a minimum wage is likely to reduce employment.

A national minimum wage may not reduce employment, and could even raise it, if employers have monopsony power. A monopsony hires where MC = MRP and pays a wage below MRP. A minimum wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage flattens its marginal cost, so it can hire more workers without raising the wage for existing ones, and employment rises. Care homes in areas dominated by one or two employers could fit this case. Even then, if the minimum wage is set above the competitive wage, employment is likely to fall again. A minimum wage therefore may not reduce employment when firms have market power.

Overall, a minimum wage is more likely to reduce employment when the labour market is competitive and the wage is set well above equilibrium. In markets with monopsony power, a modest minimum wage could raise both pay and jobs. The answer mainly depends on how much market power employers hold and on the level at which the wage is set, which could be hard to know in practice.

Common mistakeAssuming the answer is always "yes, unemployment". Use both diagrams.

Top tips

  • Multiplier calculations: add all the leakages (MPS, MRT, MPM) first, then k = 1 ÷ MPW. Multiply k by the injection.
  • State that the multiplier needs spare capacity, otherwise the extra spending is inflationary. It also takes time to work through.
  • Accelerator versus multiplier. The multiplier links income to injections. The accelerator links investment to the change in income.
  • Monopsony: hire where MC = MRP, then read the wage off the supply curve. The gap between MRP and the wage is the shortfall in pay.
  • Draw the wage floor as a flat line up to the supply curve in a monopsony diagram. That is what flattens MC.
  • Minimum wage evaluation: competitive market, jobs fall. Monopsony, jobs may rise up to the competitive wage, then fall.
  • Economic rent is earnings above transfer earnings. The less elastic the supply, the larger the rent.
  • Label axes and equilibrium points (W₁, Q₁, W₂, Q₂) every time.
