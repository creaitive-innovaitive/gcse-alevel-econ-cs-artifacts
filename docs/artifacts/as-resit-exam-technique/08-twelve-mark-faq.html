12-Mark Questions: Five Common Questions

The decisions students keep asking about, answered

1. Do I need to evaluate every paragraph?

No. Evaluation is credited where it is developed and supported, not counted per paragraph. Two workable models:

ModelHow it worksBest for
A. Dedicated evaluation paragraphParagraphs 1 and 2 are pure analysis. Paragraph 3 is all evaluation (two CIT blocks and a weighing sentence). Conclusion follows.Anyone getting NAQ or vague comments. It keeps the two skills separate and it is hard to forget evaluation.
B. Embedded evaluationEach analysis paragraph ends with one to two sentences of evaluation, then a judgement in the conclusion.Confident writers who can weigh in the moment. Riskier: the evaluation is often too thin to score.

Whichever you pick, two rules hold. Do not analyse inside the evaluation (no new mechanisms), and do not evaluate inside the analysis until the paragraph's chain is finished. Mixing them makes both harder to mark.

2. Is an introduction needed?

Not as a separate paragraph. Marks are for analysis and evaluation, and an introduction earns neither. Use one or two sentences at most, and only in two cases: to define a term the whole question turns on (for example "merit good" or "Marshall-Lerner condition"), or to name the scope when the stem contains ALL / ALWAYS / ONLY. Never restate the question or say what you are about to do.

3. The five most common evaluation moves

These are the routes that recur across past papers and mark schemes. They are ordered by how widely they apply, which is my judgement, not a statistic.

#MoveWhat to writeExample stem
1ElasticityThe outcome depends on how responsive demand or supply is. State which way, and what happens to the result."This depends on the PED; if inelastic, the tax may barely reduce consumption."
2TimeShort-run and long-run effects differ, because elasticity rises over time, firms adjust, or lags delay the effect."In the short run..., but in the long run..., because..."
3MagnitudeWhether the effect is large enough to matter, or is small and offset by something else."The increase may be too small to outweigh the cost to..."
4Type / contextIt works for one kind of economy, market or group but not another (developed vs developing, competitive vs monopoly)."This is more likely in [type] than [type], because..."
5Trade-off and alternativesFixing one problem creates another, or a different policy would do it better. Say who gains, who loses, and compare."Although it reduces inflation, it may raise unemployment; regulation may be more effective because..."

These are the same five levers as in the Evaluation Framework page. A sixth that does appear, especially for policy questions, is practicality: time lags, poor information, cost to administer, or political constraints. It is worth using where the question asks whether a government should act.

4. Does the structure change by question type?

The three levels stay the same. What changes is the weighting inside them. Since 2023 nearly every 12-marker opens with Assess: "Assess whether..." or "Assess the extent to which..." (see page 7 for how the two differ). Explain and Analyse usually carry lower marks and only need the Level 2 analysis paragraph.

Question typeLevel 1: essay shapeLevel 2: paragraph emphasisLevel 3: sentence focus
Assess whether...Two analysis paragraphs (FOR then AGAINST), evaluation, conclusion. Equal weight to both sides.Both analysis paragraphs fully developed. Evaluation weighs the two sides against each other.Analytical stems in 1 and 2. Compare stems ("more significant than...") in 3.
Assess the extent to which...Shorter analysis, longer evaluation. Judgement carries most of the marks.Analysis paragraphs can be about 5 lines each. Evaluation gets two full CIT blocks and a firm weighing sentence.Condition and if/then stems dominate. Conclusion must state an extent ("to a large / limited extent").
"Should..." / "Best way..." / policy questionsParagraph 1 the policy's case, paragraph 2 a rival policy or its limits, then evaluation on practicality and alternatives, then conclusion.Analysis paragraph 2 compares against a named alternative, not just criticises.Alternatives and trade-off stems: "A more effective way would be..., because..."

Evaluation on what explains the difference.The two analysis paragraphs are contrasting cases, not for/against arguments about one case.Type/context stems: "This is more likely to apply to [type], because..."

5. Do I need real-life examples? How do I show application?

Named real-life examples are not mandatory. The levels-based mark scheme rewards explanations "supported by examples, where appropriate," and marks down examples that are vague, over-generalised or wrong. What is required is that the analysis is applied to what the question names: the country type, the good, the policy, the group. Choose one case in your plan, carry it through every paragraph, name the stakeholders, and use features of the product or market. Use a real example only where you are sure of the facts and can connect it to the argument. Full detail and a model answer are on Application (page 5).

Quick self-check on any 12-marker
Have I done what the command word asks (Assess whether = a verdict on the claim, Assess the extent = a degree)?
Did I address the scope word, if there was one?
Does my evaluation add a condition not already in the analysis?
Does my conclusion reuse the question's words and name the condition under which it would change?
