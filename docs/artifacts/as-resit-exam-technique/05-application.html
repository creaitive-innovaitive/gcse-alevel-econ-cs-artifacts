Application in Essay Questions

CIE AS and A Level Economics — how to make your economics belong to this question, not any question

What application is, and the one test
Application means making the economics specific to the situation in front of you: this industry, this product, these people, this country. Cambridge does not list application as a separate assessment objective. It sits inside AO1 ("apply knowledge and understanding to economic information") and AO2 (analysis using economic information), so it is rewarded through the analysis marks. The June 2024 examiner report puts it plainly: knowledge and theory alone is credited as AO1, and "Level AO2 responses must be applied." The test: could this sentence appear word for word in an answer about a different market? If yes, it is not applied yet.

1. When there is an extract or context

Two jobs, in this order. First, use the extract's own language and figures. Second, go beyond the extract using what you know about that type of industry and product.

Job A: use the extract, without copying it

DoHow
Use its key termsPick two or three words or phrases that are specific to the case (the product, the policy name, the group affected) and build them into your own sentences.
Use its numbersQuote the figure with its unit and date, then do something with it: a percentage change, a comparison, a calculation of PED. Cambridge examiners have stated that simply reporting the data, without making use of it, earns no credit.
Cross-reference
(IMPORTANT)		Where there are several extracts, tables or figures, link two of them in one sentence. It shows you have read the whole case, not one paragraph.

Job B: develop the idea in context, beyond the extract

(IMPORTANT - This gets you above the grade C level writing)

Ask five questions about the case and let each one point you to an economic idea.

Ask about...What it unlocks
The industryNumber of firms and barriers (market structure), high fixed costs (economies of scale), perishable or storable (PES), labour or capital intensive (cost pressures, unemployment effects).
The product typeNecessity or luxury (PED, YED), addictive (inelastic demand), durable (demand fluctuates with confidence), merit or demerit, many or few substitutes.
The stakeholdersConsumers (which income group?), workers, producers (large or small?), government, foreign buyers and sellers. Say who gains, who loses and by how much.
The place and timeDeveloped or developing, large or small, open or closed economy, recession or boom, short run or long run.
Real-world evidenceA named example that supports or contradicts what the extract says (see section 2 for how to choose one).

Weak versus applied

Illustrative extract for practice, not real data: "The government of Country X plans a 20% tax on sugary drinks. Producers say most sales are to teenagers, and that they will absorb part of the tax."

Generic "A tax makes the good more expensive so people buy less. This reduces consumption and helps the government raise revenue."
Could be about any taxed good. Nothing from the extract, no product features, no stakeholder.
Applied "The 20% tax raises the cost of producing each sugary drink, shifting supply left. Because most sales are to teenagers, who have low incomes and many alternatives such as water and juice, demand is likely to be price elastic, so the extract's claim that producers will 'absorb part of the tax' matters: the smaller the price rise passed on, the smaller the fall in consumption. Producers therefore lose profit, while the government's revenue depends on how far quantity falls."

Illustrative question for practice: "Discuss whether the market alone provides enough education." Context: in Country X, schools charge fees and rural households spend a large share of income on food and housing.

Generic "Education is a merit good, so the market under-provides it. This is market failure, so the government should provide it."
Correct label, but no mechanism, no diagram reference, and nothing about Country X or who is affected.
Applied "Schooling raises workers' productivity and lowers crime, benefits that reach people who did not pay the fees, so the social benefit curve lies above the private benefit curve. Rural households in Country X, where fees take a large share of income, buy fewer years of schooling than the social optimum, and parents may also underestimate the lifetime return to education. Consumption settles at Q1 instead of Qs, creating a welfare loss, which is the case for subsidies or free state schools targeted at rural areas."

Illustrative question for practice: "Explain why a consumer buys less of a good as its price rises." Context: a commuter buys a $3 coffee each morning.

Generic "Marginal utility falls as you consume more, so consumers are willing to pay less and demand slopes downward."
A definition with no case. Could be about any good.
Applied "The first coffee of the morning gives the commuter high satisfaction, but a second an hour later adds much less, so marginal utility has fallen. Because the commuter will only pay up to the value of the extra satisfaction, they will buy a second coffee only if the price falls below what it is worth to them. If coffee rises from $3 to $4, the commuter drops the marginal cup first, so quantity demanded falls, giving a downward-sloping demand curve."

Where it goes: apply in the mechanism step of every analysis paragraph, and in the condition step of every evaluation block. Do not save it for one "context" paragraph.

2. When there is no extract

Essay questions often give you nothing except the stem. The application marks are still available, but you supply the context yourself. Do it early: choose one case at the planning stage and carry it through the whole answer.

What Cambridge actually requires (checked against official documents)
Real-life named examples are not mandatory in the 8, 12 or 20 mark answers. The levels-based mark scheme (2023 specimen, Paper 2 (b), Level 3) says explanations are "supported by examples, where appropriate." Level 2 describes examples that are "limited, over-generalised or contain inaccuracies." So an example can lift an answer, and a vague or wrong one does not.

What is required is that analysis is applied to what the question names.
  • In the June 2024 report on 9708/22 Question 5(b), the discussion was "poorly applied to a developing economy," which was mentioned "in passing."
  • ALL economies means ALL economies, not AN or THE economy.
An example is required only when the question asks for one (9708/21 Question 2(a): "explain, with the use of examples"), and then it must be correct and precise. "Food" as a merit good was marked down as generic (9708/22 Question 2).

Sources: Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus 2026–2028 (assessment objectives, Paper 2 description); 9708/02 specimen mark scheme for 2023; June 2024 Principal Examiner Report, Papers 9708/21 and 9708/22. I could not retrieve the current-year mark schemes or the Paper 4 (20-mark essay) mark scheme, so check your own session's mark scheme for the exact bands.
TechniqueWhat to write
1. Choose a case in the planBefore paragraph 1, write "Case: ..." at the top of your plan. A named product, industry or country (petrol, airlines, tea exports, the UK, Kenya). Use it in every paragraph so the answer reads as about something.
2. Use a real example where it helps (optional)Name the policy or event and one true fact about it, and connect it to the argument. Approximate rather than invent: "roughly," "several years later." A wrong example is marked down, and a generic one earns nothing. If you are not sure of the facts, use techniques 3 to 6 instead.
3. Name the stakeholdersSay who is affected and how differently: low-income households, small firms, exporters, the government budget.
4. Use product or market features"Because cigarettes are addictive," "because fresh food is perishable," "because the industry has few large firms." These are application even without a case study.
5. Use simple hypothetical numbers"If the price rises from $10 to $12, a 20% increase..." Label as hypothetical. It shows the mechanism working and earns credit for quantifying.
6. Label diagrams for your case"Price of cigarettes ($)" rather than "P," and mark the actual tax or price change on the diagram. See section 3.

3. Other ways to earn application marks

RouteHow it works
Diagrams in contextLabel axes and curves with the actual good, market or economy. Add figures where the question gives them. A generic labelled diagram gets less than a context-specific one, and a diagram never referred to in the text gets almost nothing.
Calculations with the dataWhere figures are given, calculate: percentage change, PED, the multiplier, revenue. State the result and say what it means for this case ("PED of -0.4, so demand is inelastic, so...").
Industry vocabularyUse the terms that would appear in a business news report on this topic (levy, tariff, base rate, output gap, bailout) alongside the economic term. It shows the concept is attached to reality.
Scope words in the questionIf the stem says "a developing economy," "a small firm," or "in the short run," build that condition into the argument, not only the conclusion.
Two-sided stakeholdersFor any policy, name a group that gains and a group that loses. It also feeds straight into evaluation, so one habit earns both marks.
Application in the conclusionReuse the case: "For a country like Country X, where..., the tax is likely to..." It shows the judgement is about this case.
What loses application marks
  • Pasting whole sentences from the extract.
  • Listing an example with no explanation of why it fits.
  • Facts you are not sure of.
  • "The economy," "people," "the government" with no specifics.
  • Application written as a separate paragraph, cut off from the argument.

Practice

1. Rewrite to apply. Take this generic sentence and make it specific to a market of your choice (name it): "A subsidy lowers costs so firms produce more and prices fall."

2. Paper 4, 20 marks (syllabus 7.4, externalities). No extract is given, so you supply the context.

"Evaluate the view that the government should always intervene to reduce the negative externalities of production." [20]

a) Choose one industry and one country as your case (for example, coal-fired electricity, chemical manufacturing, or a factory near a river). Write it here:

b) List three specific features of that industry or country that will make your analysis applied (stakeholders affected, type of pollution, size of the industry, income level of the country).

c) The question contains the scope word always. Name one situation where intervention would not be justified, and say what makes it different.

d) Write one applied analysis paragraph (with a labelled diagram of your case) and one applied evaluation paragraph.

Model answer: Paper 4, 20 marks

"Evaluate the view that the government should always intervene to reduce the negative externalities of production."

Case chosen in the plan: coal-fired electricity generation in a developing economy, called Country X. Figures are hypothetical. Bracketed labels show where each skill is earned and would not be written in the exam. Application is shown in red.

Opener [scope]
"Always" means every case, so the question is whether there is any coal plant, region or period in Country X where intervention is not justified. Here the negative externality is the cost of illness and crop damage that generators impose on nearby households and farmers without paying for it.

Analysis 1: why intervention can be justified
Coal-fired power stations in Country X, the country's main source of electricity, emit sulphur dioxide and particles that cause respiratory illness in nearby households and damage crops downwind [application: industry, stakeholders]. Generators pay only their private costs of fuel, labour and plant, so the marginal private cost (MPC) curve lies below the marginal social cost (MSC) curve by the marginal external cost, the cost of illness and crop damage per megawatt-hour [diagram: MPC, MSC, MSB = demand, axes "Price of electricity ($/MWh)" and "Quantity of electricity (MWh)"]. Left to the market, Country X's generators produce Q1 megawatt-hours, where MPC meets demand from households and industry, but the socially optimum output is Qs, where MSC meets demand. On every megawatt-hour between Qs and Q1, the illness and crop damage it causes (say $20) exceeds the value the electricity gives to its buyers, so there is likely to be a deadweight welfare loss, the triangle between MSC and demand, that represents lost health and farm output. The households and farmers breathing the emissions are neither buyers nor sellers in the electricity market, so the price may not signal the damage to generators, and only Country X's government, through a tax or emissions limit on coal-fired generators, could make them account for it [analysis: chain of reasoning to the question].

Analysis 2: how intervention works
Suppose Country X's government imposes a tax of $20 per megawatt-hour on coal-fired generators, equal to the marginal external cost (hypothetical figure). This raises generators' costs so that MPC plus tax equals MSC, the supply of electricity shifts left, the price paid by households and factories is likely to rise and output to fall from Q1 to Qs, removing the welfare loss [hypothetical numbers, labelled]. Alternatively, it could set a limit on each plant's sulphur dioxide emissions or fund cleaner sources such as solar power, which changes the technology instead of the price [alternatives].

Evaluation: when intervention is not always justified
The first condition is the size of the external cost relative to the cost of intervening [magnitude]. If Country X had a few small plants in a remote area, the health damage to nearby villages might be less than the cost of inspecting and enforcing, so intervention would waste resources and would not be justified. The second is information: the tax only removes the welfare loss if Country X's government measures the cost of illness and crop damage accurately, and Country X's environment agency has few inspectors and limited monitoring equipment. If it sets $20 when the true cost is $10, electricity output falls below Qs, so households and factories go without power that was worth more to them than the damage it caused, and a new welfare loss appears [limitation of the model]. The third is the impact on stakeholders. Electricity is a necessity with inelastic demand, so a tax is passed on largely to households, hitting low-income families hardest and raising costs for exporters, which may cost jobs in export factories in the short run [type of economy, trade-off]. The fourth is time. Generators can build solar or gas capacity only slowly, so electricity output falls little in the first years, while over ten years the tax may encourage investment in renewables [time]. Finally, private solutions can sometimes work: where villagers near a plant had clear rights to clean air and could negotiate with its owner, emissions might be cut without the government, though in Country X the large number of affected households makes this unlikely [alternative to intervention].

Conclusion
The government should usually intervene to reduce the negative externalities of coal-fired electricity in Country X, because the health and crop damage is large and spread across many households who may be unable to negotiate a solution. However, it should not always intervene: where a plant's emissions do little damage relative to the cost of inspecting it, or the environment agency may be unable to measure that damage, a tax risks creating a new welfare loss in the electricity market. The most significant condition is the size of the external cost, because if it is large enough, the case for action outweighs higher electricity bills for low-income households and job losses in export factories [judgement using the question's wording, with a condition].
