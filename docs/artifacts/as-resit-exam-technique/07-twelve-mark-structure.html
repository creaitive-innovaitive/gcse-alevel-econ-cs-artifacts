The 12-Mark Question: Three Levels of Structure

AS Economics 9708 Paper 2 — from whole essay, to paragraph, to sentence

How to use this page
Level 1 tells you what to write and in what order. Level 2 tells you how to build each paragraph. Level 3 gives you the sentence patterns that fill the paragraphs. Work top-down when planning, bottom-up when practising. Aim for about 18–20 minutes per 12-marker, and spend the first 2 of them planning. For a fully applied 20-mark model answer, see Application (page 5).

Running example used on every level: "Discuss whether a government should use an indirect tax to reduce the consumption of cigarettes."

Level 1 — Essay structure

Four paragraphs, with an optional two-line opener. Analysis and evaluation are both marked, so give both real space. Check the exact band split in the mark scheme you are working from.

ParagraphWhat it containsRough size
Opener (optional)One definition only if the question turns on a term, or one sentence naming the scope (especially if ALL / ALWAYS is in the stem). Do not restate the question.1–2 sentences
1. Analysis: the case FORThe strongest argument in favour of the claim, as a developed chain of reasoning, with a labelled diagram if the topic allows one.6–8 lines
2. Analysis: the case AGAINSTA second, different argument: a limitation of the policy, an unintended effect, or an alternative route to the same aim. Also a developed chain, not a one-liner.6–8 lines
3. EvaluationTwo conditions the judgement depends on (pick from the five levers), each written as Condition → If → Then. Ends by weighing one against the other.8–10 lines
4. ConclusionA direct answer to the question in its own words, the main reason, and the condition under which the answer would change. No new content.2–3 sentences
Short on time?
Drop the opener, keep paragraph 1 and 3 in full, and squeeze paragraph 2 to four lines. Never drop the conclusion: it is where the "answers the question" credit sits.

Level 2 — Paragraph structure

Analysis paragraph (used for paragraphs 1 and 2)

PartJob
PointOne sentence stating the argument, using the wording of the question.
MechanismThe economic reason it happens. Name the concept (supply, elasticity, externality, AD/AS). Refer to your diagram here. Apply it now: use the case's own words, numbers and stakeholders (see Application, page 5).
Consequence chainTwo or three linked steps: this causes that, which causes the next thing. Apply to the context (cigarettes, this country, this data).
LinkOne sentence tying the result back to the question. No judgement yet.
Analysis, FOR An indirect tax could reduce cigarette consumption. The tax raises the cost of supplying each pack, shifting supply left from S to S+tax, so the market price rises and quantity demanded contracts along the demand curve from Q1 to Q2. Because smoking imposes external costs on non-smokers and the health service, the market over-produces relative to the social optimum, so the fall in output moves the market towards Qs, reducing the welfare loss. The tax therefore helps correct the market failure the question is about.
Analysis, AGAINST However, a tax may achieve little in this market. Cigarettes are addictive, so demand is price inelastic: a given price rise causes a proportionately smaller fall in quantity, so consumption barely moves while consumer spending on cigarettes rises. Because low-income households spend a larger share of income on cigarettes, the tax falls hardest on them, so the policy is regressive and the reduction in externalities may be small relative to the cost to poorer smokers.

Evaluation paragraph (paragraph 3)

PartJob
CIT oneCondition (lever) → If it holds → Then this outcome for the claim, because [mechanism].
CIT twoA different lever, same three steps. Must add something new, not restate paragraphs 1 and 2.
WeighOne sentence saying which condition matters more in this context, and why.
Evaluation The tax's success depends first on the size of the price elasticity. If demand is very inelastic for long-term smokers, the fall in quantity is likely to be small, so the tax may mainly raise revenue rather than cut consumption. It also depends on the time period. In the short run consumption may barely change, but over several years the higher price deters young people from starting, so quantity demanded falls further as new smokers do not replace old ones. The time effect is likely to matter more here because most smoking begins in the teenage years, where demand is more responsive to price than among established smokers.

Conclusion

PartJob
AnswerReuse the question's wording: "A government should / should not use an indirect tax to reduce cigarette consumption..."
BecauseThe single strongest reason from your evaluation.
UnlessThe condition under which your answer would flip.
Conclusion A government should use an indirect tax to reduce cigarette consumption, but only as part of a wider package. The tax's greatest strength is its long-run effect on young people, which outweighs the weak short-run effect on addicted smokers. The answer would change if demand stayed inelastic across all age groups, in which case education campaigns or regulation would be a better tool.

Level 3 — Writing analytical and evaluative sentences

Analytical sentences: cause → mechanism → consequence

An analytical sentence shows how something happens. The test: could you insert "because" or "which leads to" and keep going? If the sentence just states a fact or a result, it is not yet analysis.

Sentence stems for analysis
"[Policy / event] causes [variable] to [rise / fall] because [reason]."
"This leads to [next effect], which in turn [final effect]."
"As a result, [quantity / price / output] moves from [X] to [Y]."
"This matters in [context] because [specific feature of the market or economy]."
"Therefore, [link to the question wording]."
Level of sentenceExample
Too vague"The tax would make cigarettes more expensive so people would smoke less."
Analysis"The tax raises the marginal cost of supplying each pack, shifting supply left, which raises the equilibrium price and contracts quantity demanded along the demand curve."

Evaluative sentences: condition → judgement

An evaluative sentence does not explain how something happens; it says when it happens, how much, or whether it is the best option. The test: does the sentence name a condition that could be true or false? If it does, it is evaluation. If not, it is more analysis.

Sentence stems for evaluation
Condition: "This depends on [lever], because..."
If / then: "If [condition holds], then [outcome], because [mechanism]."
Limit: "This is only true where..."  |  "This assumes that..., which may not hold because..."
Compare: "This is more significant than [X] because..."  |  "This is more likely to apply to [type] than [type], because..."
Time: "In the short run..., but in the long run..., because..."
Level of sentenceExample
Not evaluation"The tax would raise revenue for the government, which it could spend on health."
Too vague"Overall it depends on how many people smoke and other factors."
Evaluation"The tax is only effective if demand is price elastic; for addicted smokers it is inelastic, so consumption falls by proportionately less than the price rises and the policy mainly raises revenue."

Practice

Question: "Discuss whether a government should use a minimum price to reduce the consumption of alcohol."

1. Write one analytical sentence using the stem "causes [variable] to rise/fall because...".

2. Write one evaluative sentence using "This depends on..., because...".

3. Write the conclusion in three sentences (Answer / Because / Unless).
