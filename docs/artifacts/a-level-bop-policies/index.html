Policies to Correct Disequilibrium in the Balance of Payments

The accounts that must add up to zero, the five policy families a government can use, and the difference between switching spending and reducing it. Includes the costs and limits of each.

The big idea

The balance of payments records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world. Because every credit has a matching debit, the accounts add up to zero. But the individual accounts rarely balance: a deficit on the current account is matched by a surplus on the financial and capital accounts. A disequilibrium means one account, usually the current account, is persistently out of line. Governments then choose between expenditure-switching and expenditure-reducing policies to correct it.

1 · The components of the balance of payments

Current account

Trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income (Chapter 27). The account most exam questions on policy focus on.

Financial account

Movements of funds into and out of the country: direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment and reserve assets. For some countries it is the biggest part of the balance of payments.

Capital account

Usually small. Non-produced, non-financial assets: debt forgiveness, money brought in or taken out by migrants, sales and purchases of copyrights, patents, trademarks and mineral rights.

Net errors and omissions

The balancing item. Mistakes and missing transactions mean the other accounts do not quite sum to zero, so a figure is added to make the total zero. It usually shrinks as data improves.

Current account + capital account + financial account + net errors and omissions = 0
If the first three add up to −$2,000m, net errors and omissions = +$2,000m

Financial account deficit or surplus: good or bad?

A financial account surplus

  • Can finance a current account deficit.
  • May create jobs and raise growth as foreign firms and investors enter.
  • In the long run, profits, interest and dividends flow out, which can push the primary income balance into deficit.

A financial account deficit

  • Not necessarily a problem: it brings future inflows of investment income.
  • May be short term, such as hot money chasing higher interest rates elsewhere.
  • A real concern if it reflects lost confidence and capital flight, which cuts tax revenue, jobs and growth, and may cause the currency to depreciate.

Surplus or deficit?Fiscal, monetary and exchange rate policy can be used against either a deficit or a surplus (use the opposite tool). Supply-side and protectionist policy are normally used only to reduce a deficit.

3 · Expenditure-switching versus expenditure-reducing

Expenditure-switching

Any action designed to persuade buyers, at home and abroad, to buy more of this country's goods and less of other countries'. It does not aim to cut total spending, only to redirect it. Intended effect: import expenditure falls, export earnings rise.

Typical tools: tariffs, quotas, subsidies, a lower exchange rate, supply-side policy.

Expenditure-reducing

Any action designed to reduce total spending in the economy. Spending on imports falls, and domestic firms that lose home sales may try harder to export.

Typical tools: contractionary fiscal policy (tax up, spending down) and contractionary monetary policy (interest rates up, credit limits).

The testAsk: what does this do to total spending? If total spending stays the same but moves from imports to home goods, it is switching. If total spending falls, it is reducing. A limit on bank lending is reducing, even though it sounds like a trade policy.

4 · Evaluating policy to correct a deficit

  • Cause of the deficit. A cyclical deficit from a boom may self-correct and needs no harsh policy. A structural deficit from low competitiveness needs supply-side measures.
  • Internal against external economy. Fixing the balance of payments may harm growth, employment or inflation. Always mention both.
  • Time lags and elasticities. Exchange rate and tariff effects depend on how responsive demand is, and on how quickly.
  • Retaliation. Other countries may impose their own barriers or devalue, cancelling the gain.
  • Conflict between objectives. Cutting spending reduces inflation and imports but raises unemployment. Lowering income tax to cut unemployment may suck in imports.
  • Size and state of the economy. Raising interest rates in an economy with high inflation and a positive output gap has a limited effect on unemployment.
  • Combining policies. Demand-side restraint with supply-side improvement can reduce the deficit and later help unemployment.

7 · Multiple choice

Descriptions follow the coursebook case studies and are kept general. Check current figures before quoting numbers in an exam answer.

Argentina: imports, debt and repeated crises

Argentina's current account is usually in deficit, caused largely by high spending on imports and by high-interest debt repayments. The country spends more than it produces and borrows to cover the gap. In 2020 the government tried to restructure its foreign debt and delay interest payments.

Exam use: a deficit financed by borrowing from abroad. Link to the financial account (loans inflow) and the cost: future outflows of interest in primary income. Evaluate: how serious depends on the size of the debt relative to GDP.

South Africa: goods surplus, income outflows

South Africa usually runs a surplus in trade in goods but a deficit on current account. Large past inflows of portfolio investment now produce large outflows of interest and dividends, and workers' remittances sent abroad add to the deficit. The deficit has often led to a fall in the rand.

Exam use: shows a deficit with a structural cause in primary and secondary income, and that a deficit can lower the exchange rate, which may later help exports. Self-correction is possible but not certain.

India: oil, software services and remittances

India pays a lot for imported oil, which feeds a deficit in goods. It usually earns a surplus on services, mainly software exports (to the USA, UK and EU), and a surplus on secondary income because of workers' remittances. The biggest contributor to the deficit is the deficit on primary income, from foreign investment and loans.

Exam use: a good example of different accounts pulling in different directions, and of the case for diversifying services exports so the country depends less on one product and a few markets.

Sri Lanka: protecting industries against dumping

In 2018 Sri Lanka's government announced legislation to protect domestic industries against unfair competition, in the form of dumping and foreign export subsidies, and said it would impose tariffs where harm was shown.

Exam use: a protectionist, expenditure-switching measure. Evaluate with: retaliation, higher prices for consumers, and whether good home substitutes exist.

Germany: a current account surplus mirrored by the financial account

In the coursebook's 2019 data, Germany had a large current account surplus (about €246bn) and a financial account deficit of about €220bn, as German residents invested abroad. The accounts offset each other.

Exam use: illustrates that the accounts sum to zero, and that a surplus on one account is matched by a deficit on another. A financial account deficit is not necessarily a problem.

Ghana: a falling currency and a smaller deficit

In the coursebook's Chapter 49 data, the cedi fell against other currencies in each year shown while Ghana's current account deficit got smaller. High Ghanaian inflation compared with some trading partners also reduced competitiveness.

Exam use: a link between exchange rate policy and the balance of payments. Use hedged wording: a lower exchange rate may have contributed, alongside other factors.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by the financial account of the balance of payments?
ReminderSay what it records and give at least one of its parts.
The financial account records movements of funds into and out of a country, including direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment such as bank loans, and changes in reserve assets.
Common mistakeConfusing it with the income that investment earns, which belongs in the primary income part of the current account.
Q2 Calculate 4 marks A country has a deficit of $52bn on trade in goods, a surplus of $18bn on trade in services, a deficit of $9bn on primary income and a surplus of $6bn on secondary income. Its capital account shows +$1bn and its financial account +$30bn. Calculate (a) the current account balance and (b) the net errors and omissions figure.
ReminderAdd the four parts of the current account first, then add the capital and financial accounts. Net errors and omissions is the figure that makes the total zero.
Current account = −52 + 18 − 9 + 6 = −$37bn, a deficit. Sum of the three accounts = −37 + 1 + 30 = −$6bn. Net errors and omissions = +$6bn, so that the total is zero.
Common mistakeGiving −$6bn for net errors and omissions. It must be the opposite sign to the total, to cancel it.
Q3 Distinguish 4 marks Distinguish between an expenditure-switching policy and an expenditure-reducing policy.
ReminderThe difference is the effect on total spending. Give an example of each.
An expenditure-switching policy persuades buyers to switch spending from foreign goods to home-produced goods without reducing total spending, for example an import tariff or a lower exchange rate. An expenditure-reducing policy cuts the total level of spending in the economy, so spending on imports falls with it, for example higher income tax or higher interest rates.
Common mistakeCalling a limit on bank lending or a rise in interest rates "switching". These reduce spending on everything.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how supply-side policy could help to reduce a current account deficit.
ReminderGive a measure, then the chain: productivity up → costs and prices down → exports more competitive and home goods replace imports → deficit narrows.

Supply-side policy aims to raise the quantity and quality of a country's resources. Spending on education, training and infrastructure could raise labour productivity and cut firms' costs of production, so the price of the country's goods may fall relative to foreign goods. Exports could then become more price competitive, raising export revenue, and domestic consumers may switch from imports to home-produced goods, lowering import spending, so the deficit could narrow.

Better infrastructure and skills may also attract foreign direct investment, creating a surplus on the financial account that helps to finance the deficit and could increase future exports. However, these effects may take years to appear and are uncertain, so supply-side policy is not a quick remedy. It also costs government money.

Q5 Evaluate 8 marks Evaluate the effectiveness of an increase in import tariffs as a way to correct a current account deficit.
ReminderAn 8-marker needs developed analysis and a clear judgement. Say it is expenditure-switching, then the chain, then limits.

A tariff is a tax on imports, and it is an expenditure-switching, protectionist policy. It raises the price of imports in the domestic market, so quantity demanded for imports is likely to fall and some buyers may switch to home-produced goods, lowering import expenditure and narrowing the deficit.

The effectiveness is likely to depend on whether good domestic substitutes exist. If they do not, buyers may keep buying imports at the higher price, and import spending may hardly fall. Other countries may retaliate with their own tariffs, reducing exports, and the tariff may raise costs for firms using imported raw materials, which may lower competitiveness and push up prices. Overall, a tariff is likely to reduce the deficit only in the short run and where substitutes are close, and it may be less effective than supply-side policy for a structural deficit.

Q6 Assess 12 marks Assess the extent to which expenditure-reducing policies are the best way to correct a current account deficit.
Reminder"The extent to which" means size the effect. Both sides, with the evaluation judging how far, and what decides it. 8 marks analysis, 4 marks evaluation.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Expenditure-reducing policies, such as higher taxes, lower government spending and higher interest rates, aim to reduce total spending in the economy, including spending on imports.

Expenditure-reducing policies could be an effective way to correct a current account deficit because they cut spending on imports. If the government raises income tax, disposable income falls, so consumers buy fewer imports, and firms that lose home sales may try to sell more abroad. The deficit could therefore narrow. This is most likely to work when the deficit is caused by a boom pulling in imports, but the effect on imports may be small if the marginal propensity to import is low.

They may not be the best way because they cut spending on home goods too, so output and employment may fall and economic growth slows, which could lower living standards. Expenditure-switching policies such as a lower exchange rate or supply-side policy aim to switch spending to home goods without cutting total spending, so they may correct the deficit with less damage to jobs. However, these have their own limits: a lower exchange rate may raise import prices, and supply-side policy takes time.

Overall, expenditure-reducing policies are best to a limited extent: they are likely to be useful for a cyclical deficit caused by a boom, particularly alongside high inflation, but a poor choice for a structural deficit or an economy with high unemployment. The extent depends on the cause of the deficit and the state of the economy, so a combination with supply-side policy is likely to be better.

Q7 Evaluate 20 marks Expenditure-reducing policies will reduce a balance of payments deficit but will also cause significant unemployment. Evaluate this statement. (Paper 4, Feb/Mar 2023 Q4. Model answer written for this site.)
ReminderTwo claims to test: (1) they reduce the deficit, (2) they cause significant unemployment. Develop both, then evaluate. 14 marks analysis, 6 marks evaluation. Open each paragraph with the question's wording, end with a link back to the statement and one micro-evaluation.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Expenditure-reducing policies are policies designed to lower the total level of spending in an economy, such as contractionary fiscal policy, where the government raises taxes or cuts its own spending, and contractionary monetary policy, where the central bank raises the interest rate or limits credit. A balance of payments deficit, usually a current account deficit, occurs when the value of imports and other debit items exceeds credit items.

Expenditure-reducing policies are likely to reduce a balance of payments deficit because lower spending means lower demand for imports. For example, a rise in the interest rate raises the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving, so households cut consumption and firms cut investment, which lowers aggregate demand and spending on imported goods and capital equipment. Some firms that lose sales at home may try to sell more abroad, raising exports. A fall in domestic inflation relative to trading partners could also make exports more price competitive over time. This could be relevant for a country such as Argentina, where high spending on imports contributes to the deficit. However, this effect may be small if the marginal propensity to import is low or if imports are mainly necessities such as oil, so the deficit may fall by less than hoped. The first part of the statement could therefore be true, but only to an extent.

Expenditure-reducing policies are also likely to cause unemployment because lower aggregate demand reduces output. Firms facing falling sales may cut production and workers, and the multiplier may amplify the fall in income, so cyclical, or demand-deficient, unemployment may rise. Higher taxes and lower government spending could also cut jobs in the public sector and in industries that supply it. Whether the unemployment is significant is likely to depend on the size of the policy and on how much spare capacity there is. If the economy is already near full employment, the fall in spending may mostly reduce inflation rather than jobs. So the second part of the statement may also be true, especially for a large and sudden policy.

The extent to which the statement holds is likely to depend on the state of the economy. Raising interest rates when inflation is high and there is a positive output gap may have a limited effect on unemployment, because it mainly removes excess demand. It also depends on the cause of the existing unemployment: if it is mainly structural, contractionary demand-side policy may not make it much worse, and supply-side policies used alongside, such as training, could reduce it at the same time. Time matters too: unemployment may rise quickly, while the improvement in the deficit may take longer, and expenditure-switching policies, such as supply-side measures, could achieve the same aim with less harm to jobs, although they have their own costs and delays.

Overall, the statement is likely to be partly true. Expenditure-reducing policies are likely to reduce a deficit, especially one caused by a boom, but unemployment is likely to be significant only if the policy is large and the economy has little spare demand. For a structural deficit, they are probably a poor tool on their own, and a combination with supply-side policy may be better.

Common mistakeWriting only about the deficit, or only about unemployment. The statement has two claims, and each needs analysis and a judgement.

Top tips

  • The accounts add up to zero. A deficit on the current account is matched by a surplus on the financial and capital accounts, with net errors and omissions to tidy up.
  • Investment is in the financial account; the income it earns is in the current account.
  • Classify every policy. Does total spending change? No: switching. Yes: reducing.
  • Supply-side and protectionist policy are used for a deficit, not a surplus. Fiscal, monetary and exchange rate policy can be used for either.
  • Always mention the internal economy. Cutting a deficit with higher unemployment is a trade-off worth evaluating.
  • Say why the deficit exists. A cyclical deficit may self-correct; a structural one needs supply-side policy.
  • Use evaluation words that compare: "this is more effective than... because", "this depends on... and in this case...".
  • Hedge. "Could", "may" and "is likely to" are better than "will", especially for exchange rate and tariff effects.
