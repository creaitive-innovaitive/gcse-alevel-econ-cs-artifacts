The Liquidity Trap

Why cutting interest rates to zero can stop working, what bonds have to do with it, and how quantitative easing tries to fix it.

The idea in one paragraph

A liquidity trap is when interest rates are at or near 0% and people still do not spend. They hold cash because they expect bad times or falling prices, and bond traders sell bonds because they expect interest rates to rise. The usual tool for boosting the economy, cheap money, stops working.

Normal times versus the trap

Normal

Low interest rate→Less saving→More borrowing and spending→More money flowing→GDP grows

Liquidity trap

Interest rate at 0%→People still do not spend→Fear, no confidence→GDP does not rise

How the trap happens

  1. Bad times arriveRecession or a crisis. Banks struggle with liquidity (cash).
  2. The central bank cuts interest ratesPurpose: to stimulate the economy in a difficult period.
  3. People expect the worstDeflation, a long recession or depression, and a basic lack of confidence in the finance industry.
  4. Nobody spends, even at 0%All the efforts to create growth fail. GDP does not increase.
  5. The next step is printing moneyQuantitative easing (QE), covered below. The aim is to solve the low-cash problem so businesses survive and consumption returns.

Bonds: why traders sell when rates are low

This is the hard part, so take it in small pieces. A bond is a loan to the government. The buyer pays a price, and the government pays a fixed interest amount on it every year.

Worked example

£10,000 bond at 5% interest. The rate is written into the contract and cannot change. It pays £500 a year.

Suppose people expect market interest rates to rise to 8%. Nobody wants a 5% bond when 8% is on offer, so the price of the old bond has to fall for it to be worth buying, for example to £9,000. That is the price on the market between traders. It has nothing to do with the government.

Rule to remember: interest rates rise, bond prices fall. Interest rates fall, bond prices rise.

What "selling a bond" means

Two routes. (1) Hold it until the government pays it back. (2) Sell it on the bond market to another buyer, and the government cannot control that price.

New bonds

The government can issue new bonds on its own terms, for example at £8,000 with a higher interest rate, or at £11,000. This moves the whole market. If people paid £10,000 and the government then sells at £8,000, existing holders would rush to sell.

Now the trap
Interest rate very low→Traders expect it to rise→They expect bond prices to fall→They sell bonds→They hold cash instead

Holding cash is sensible if you think the asset you want will get cheaper. That can be because market prices are falling, or because the government announces cheaper bonds in future. So the money sits idle, and extra money supply does not lower rates or lift spending.

Quantitative easing (QE)

Japan used QE after it had cut interest rates about as far as they would go. QE is often described as "printing money", but today it is digital.

  1. The central bank creates money electronicallySimplified: it adds a zero to its balance. In the past this meant printing more cash. Now it means adding to the spreadsheet.
  2. It uses that money to buy bondsThe government issues bonds, the central bank buys them, and the government spends the proceeds.
  3. Money enters the economySpending rises, and if it rises enough, inflation returns. That is the aim when prices are falling.

Worth checking before the exam

Points where the lesson shorthand needs tightening. Ask your tutor if unsure.
  • The "£9,000" example is a fair illustration, but the exact price depends on the years left to maturity. The direction (rates up, price down) is the examinable point.
  • Most central banks buy bonds from banks, pension funds and other investors on the market, not directly from the government. Buying directly from the government is possible in principle but usually avoided.
  • QE does not guarantee inflation. In a trap the whole difficulty is that extra money can be held idle, which is why QE has often been slow to raise inflation.
  • The note "Stagflation is linked" needs care. Stagflation is high inflation with weak growth. A liquidity trap usually comes with low inflation or deflation, so the link is loose. Ask what the intended connection was.

Revision questions

What is a liquidity trap?

Interest rates are at or near 0%, and people prefer to hold cash rather than spend or invest, so monetary policy cannot raise GDP.

Why do bond prices fall when interest rates rise?

Existing bonds pay a fixed rate. If new bonds pay more, nobody will pay full price for the old ones, so their price falls until the return matches the market.

Why do traders sell bonds when rates are very low?

They expect rates to rise, which means bond prices will fall. They sell to avoid the loss and hold cash.

How does QE work, and why might it fail in a trap?

The central bank creates digital money and buys bonds, adding money to the economy. It can fail if banks and households hold the extra money as idle cash instead of lending and spending.

Why is deflation dangerous when interest rates are already zero?

Falling prices make cash more valuable, so people delay spending. The real interest rate (nominal minus inflation) rises, and the central bank cannot cut the nominal rate further.
