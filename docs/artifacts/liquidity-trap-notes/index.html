The Liquidity Trap

Why cutting interest rates to zero can stop working, what bonds have to do with it, and how quantitative easing tries to fix it.

The idea in one paragraph

A liquidity trap is when interest rates are at or near 0% and people still do not spend. They hold cash because they expect bad times or falling prices, and bond traders sell bonds because they expect interest rates to rise. The usual tool for boosting the economy, cheap money, stops working.

Normal times versus the trap

Normal

Low interest rate → less saving → more borrowing and spending → more money flowing → GDP grows.

Liquidity trap

Interest rate at 0% → people still do not spend → fear and no confidence → GDP does not rise.

How the trap happens

1. Bad times arrive. A recession or a crisis. Banks struggle with liquidity (cash).

2. The central bank cuts interest rates to stimulate the economy in a difficult period.

3. People expect the worst: deflation, a long recession, and a lack of confidence in the finance industry.

4. Nobody spends, even at 0%. All the efforts to create growth fail and GDP does not increase.

5. The next step is printing money (quantitative easing), covered below.

Bonds: why traders sell when rates are low

A bond is a loan to the government. The buyer pays a price, and the government pays a fixed amount of interest on it every year.

Worked example. A £10,000 bond at 5% interest pays £500 a year, and the rate is fixed in the contract. Suppose people expect market interest rates to rise to 8%. Nobody wants a 5% bond when 8% is on offer, so the price of the old bond has to fall for it to be worth buying, for example to £9,000. That is a price between traders on the market. It has nothing to do with the government.

Interest rates rise → bond prices fall. Interest rates fall → bond prices rise.

What "selling a bond" means

Two routes: hold it until the government pays it back, or sell it on the bond market to another buyer. The government cannot control the market price.

New bonds

The government can issue new bonds on its own terms, for example at £8,000 with a higher interest rate, or at £11,000. This moves the whole market. If people paid £10,000 and the government then sells at £8,000, existing holders would rush to sell.

Now the trap

Interest rate very low → traders expect it to rise → they expect bond prices to fall → they sell bonds → they hold cash instead. Holding cash is sensible if you think the asset you want will get cheaper. So the money sits idle, and extra money supply does not lower rates or lift spending.

Quantitative easing (QE)

Japan used QE after it had cut interest rates about as far as they would go. QE is often described as "printing money", but today it is digital.

1. Creates money

The central bank creates money electronically. In the past this meant printing more cash. Now it means adding to a balance sheet.

2. Buys bonds

The central bank buys bonds, so their prices rise and long-term yields fall. The government can then borrow more cheaply.

3. Money enters the economy

Spending rises and, if it rises enough, inflation returns. That is the aim when prices are falling.

Worth checking before the exam

Tighten the shorthand

The £9,000 example is a fair illustration, but the exact price depends on the years left to maturity. The direction (rates up, price down) is the examinable point.

Most central banks buy bonds from banks, pension funds and other investors on the market, not directly from the government.

QE does not guarantee inflation. In a trap the whole difficulty is that extra money can be held idle, which is why QE has often been slow to raise inflation.

Stagflation is high inflation with weak growth. A liquidity trap usually comes with low inflation or deflation, so the link is loose.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms so they stay true. Look up current figures before quoting numbers in an exam.

Japan from the late 1990s

After an asset price crash, the central bank cut rates to almost zero. Households held cash, firms repaid debt, and prices fell year after year. Money grew, but spending did not, and QE followed.

Use it for: the trap, deflation and the limits of cheap money.

The USA and the UK after 2008

Rates were cut to the floor and central banks bought government bonds on a large scale. Many economists think QE stabilised markets and prevented a worse slump, but the effect on lending and inflation was slower than hoped.

Use it for: how QE is meant to work, and why it might not.

Pension funds and the bond market

When central banks buy bonds, prices rise and yields fall. Pension funds that hold bonds may gain on paper, but find it harder to earn a good return on new investment.

Use it for: the rate-and-price rule, and side effects of QE.

Falling prices and delayed spending

If households expect prices to be lower next year, they may delay buying large items such as cars or homes. Firms see weak sales and cut investment, which makes the downturn worse.

Use it for: why deflation is dangerous when rates cannot fall further.

Q1 Explain 2 marks Explain what is meant by a liquidity trap.
ReminderTwo ideas: the condition (rates at or near zero) and the result (money held, policy ineffective).
A liquidity trap is a situation where nominal interest rates are at or near zero and people prefer to hold money rather than bonds or spend, so extra money supply cannot lower rates or raise spending and monetary policy loses its effect.
Common mistakeConfusing it with a liquidity crisis, which is a shortage of cash for banks.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain why bond prices fall when interest rates rise.
ReminderA numerical example shows application. Describe the fixed interest and the comparison with new bonds.
An existing bond pays a fixed amount of interest, for example £500 a year on a £10,000 bond. If market interest rates rise to 8%, new bonds offer a better return, so nobody is likely to pay full price for the old bond. Its price may need to fall to around £6,250 on a simple calculation (£500 ÷ 0.08). The price falls until its return matches the market rate, so bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions.
Common mistakeSaying the government lowers the price. The price is set between traders on the market.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain how quantitative easing is intended to increase aggregate demand.
ReminderA full chain from purchase to AD is needed, not just "it increases the money supply".

Quantitative easing is where the central bank creates money electronically and uses it to buy assets, mainly government bonds. Buying bonds raises their price and lowers their yield, which is likely to reduce long-term interest rates and the cost of borrowing.

Sellers of the bonds receive cash, which they may lend or invest in other assets, raising share and property prices and creating a wealth effect. Cheaper borrowing and higher wealth could raise investment and consumption, so AD and real GDP may rise. The Bank of Japan, Bank of England and Federal Reserve all used it when policy rates were near zero.

Common mistakeWriting that QE gives money to the government. It is bought from investors in the market.
Q4 Discuss 12 marks Discuss whether a cut in interest rates will always increase spending.
ReminderOpen each paragraph with the question's wording. Finish each paragraph with a short evaluative point and a link back to the question. Finish with a judgement.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Interest rates are the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. A cut in interest rates is expected to lower borrowing costs, reduce the return on saving and so raise consumption and investment, which increases aggregate demand.

A cut in interest rates is likely to increase spending in normal times. Cheaper loans make investment projects and mortgages more affordable, and lower returns on saving encourage households to spend now. Lower rates also tend to weaken the exchange rate, which could raise exports. A fall in mortgage rates in a housing market could free up household income. However, the effect depends on how responsive spending is to the rate, which may be small if confidence is low. Spending is therefore likely to rise in normal conditions.

A cut in interest rates may not increase spending if the economy is in a liquidity trap. When rates are already at or near zero they cannot fall further, and people may prefer to hold cash because they expect deflation or a rise in rates later. Firms may repay debt rather than borrow, and falling prices raise the real interest rate. Japan's experience from the late 1990s could be an example. Other policies, such as QE and fiscal stimulus, may still help, though each has limits. A rate cut will therefore not always raise spending.

Overall, a cut in interest rates is likely to raise spending when confidence is reasonable and rates have room to fall. In a trap, or in a deep recession, the effect could be weak, so the answer depends on confidence, the level of rates and inflation expectations.

Common mistakeStopping at "interest rates fall". Always finish the chain to AD and real GDP.

Top tips

  • Rates up, bond prices down. Use the £10,000 at 5% example in answers.
  • Say why traders sell bonds when rates are very low: they expect rates to rise and prices to fall.
  • Use the real interest rate. A 0% nominal rate with falling prices is a positive real rate.
  • QE: complete the chain from purchase to yields, lending, wealth, AD and real GDP.
  • QE does not guarantee inflation. The money can be held idle.
  • Do not mix up a liquidity trap and stagflation.
