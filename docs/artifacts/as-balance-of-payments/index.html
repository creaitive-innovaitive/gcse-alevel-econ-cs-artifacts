The Current Account of the Balance of Payments

What is counted, how to calculate each balance, why a country runs a deficit or a surplus, and what that means for the economy.

The big idea

A country's balance of payments is a record of all the economic transactions between its residents and the rest of the world. Money coming in is a credit (+). Money going out is a debit (−). The account has three parts: the current account, the capital account and the financial account. This chapter is about the current account.

The four parts of the current account

1 · Trade in goods

Exports (credit) and imports (debit) of physical goods such as cars, phones and clothing. Also called the visible or merchandise balance.

2 · Trade in services

Exports and imports of services such as shipping, tourism, banking and insurance. Also called invisibles.

3 · Primary income

Income from investment and work abroad: profits, interest and dividends, plus employees' compensation (wages of residents working abroad). Receipts are credits; payments to foreigners are debits.

4 · Secondary income

Transfers with nothing received in exchange: foreign aid, payments to international organisations, government and private transfers, and workers' remittances sent home.

Calculating the balances

Balance of trade in goods = exports of goods − imports of goods
Balance of trade in services = exports of services − imports of services
Balance of trade in goods and services = goods balance + services balance
Current account balance = trade in goods and services + primary income balance + secondary income balance

Across the whole world the current accounts add up to zero, since one country's deficit is another's surplus.

Causes of a surplus

The reverse: a declining domestic economy (weak demand for imports), increasing activity in trading partners (higher demand for exports and higher remittances), and structural advantages such as good education, high investment, low inflation and a competitive exchange rate.

Consequences of imbalances

A persistent deficit

  • The country consumes more than it produces, so it is "living beyond its means".
  • It must borrow or attract investment, which leads to future outflows of interest, profits and dividends.
  • Falling net exports reduce aggregate demand, slowing growth and raising unemployment.
  • Cyclical deficits matter less than structural ones.

A persistent surplus

  • Residents are not enjoying as high a standard of living as possible.
  • High demand for exports with extra money supply may create inflationary pressure.
  • Trading partners may pressure the country to change policies.
  • A surplus caused by recession is not beneficial.
Use percentages of GDPThe size of a deficit should be judged against the size of the economy. A deficit of $108bn in a huge economy can matter less than a smaller one in a small economy, because what counts is the percentage of GDP.

7 · Multiple choice

Descriptions follow the coursebook case studies and are kept general. Check current figures before quoting numbers.

Argentina: spending more on imports than it earns

For many years Argentina has spent more on imports than it has earned from exports. To reduce the gap, the government used policy tools to encourage domestic production of substitutes for imports and to boost exports.

Exam use: a persistent deficit and the policy response. Link to structural causes, and to the cost of borrowing to finance the deficit.

South Africa: goods surplus, income outflow

South Africa usually earns more from exporting goods such as gold, diamonds and other metals than it spends on importing goods. But it often spends more on importing services than it earns from exporting them, and a large flow of dividends and interest leaves the country each year, because a high share of firms are foreign-owned and the country has borrowed heavily from abroad.

Exam use: shows why you must look at every component. A surplus in goods can be outweighed by primary income outflows.

Switzerland: surplus on goods and investment income

Switzerland earns more from its exports than it spends on imports and also receives more investment income from abroad than it sends out. The extra money coming in can put upward pressure on the exchange rate.

Exam use: an example of a structural surplus and how a surplus can affect the exchange rate. Mention the link to inflation risk.

The Philippines and workers' remittances

Many Filipinos work abroad and send money home. These workers' remittances are a large credit item in secondary income.

Exam use: a real example for secondary income. Pakistan is another case in the coursebook: remittances make a big difference to its current account position.

Comparing the UK and Albania

In 2018 the UK had one of the largest current account deficits in the world in money terms, but as a share of GDP (4.2%) it was smaller than Albania's (7.4%) even though Albania's deficit was much smaller in dollars.

Exam use: use this to explain why a deficit should be judged as a percentage of GDP.

Germany and Singapore: surpluses

In the coursebook's 2019 table, Germany's surplus was 6.6% of GDP and Singapore's was 14.3% of GDP. Strong, competitive export industries are a common cause.

Exam use: examples of structural surpluses. In evaluation, ask whether residents benefit and whether the surplus is a sign of low domestic demand.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by the current account of the balance of payments?
ReminderName the transactions and say what is measured.
The current account records a country's trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income with the rest of the world, showing the balance of money flowing in (credits) and out (debits).
Q2 Calculate 4 marks A country's exports of goods are $1,670bn and imports of goods $2,620bn. Exports of services are $828bn and imports $558bn. The balance of primary income is +$245bn and secondary income −$119bn. Calculate the balance of trade in goods and services and the current account balance.
ReminderDo it in stages and label each one. Exports minus imports, always. Keep the signs.
Goods: 1,670 − 2,620 = −$950bn. Services: 828 − 558 = +$270bn. Balance of trade in goods and services = −950 + 270 = −$680bn. Current account = −680 + 245 + (−119) = −$554bn. This is a current account deficit of $554bn.
Common mistakeForgetting to add primary and secondary income, or losing the minus sign on secondary income.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by primary income and secondary income in the current account.
ReminderTwo parts, two marks each: define it and give an example.
Primary income is income from investment and from employment abroad, such as profits, interest and dividends and wages earned by residents working in other countries. For example, dividends paid to foreign shareholders are debits. Secondary income consists of transfers for which nothing is received in exchange. Examples include foreign aid and workers' remittances sent home from abroad.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse two possible causes of a current account deficit.
ReminderFor each cause give the chain: cause → effect on exports or imports → deficit. Say whether it is cyclical or structural.

One cause is a growing domestic economy. Rising incomes may increase spending on imported goods, and firms import more raw materials and capital goods, so import expenditure rises; some output may also be diverted from export to the home market, so export revenue falls. This is a cyclical deficit, likely to be short term and self-correcting, because the imports may raise future output and exports.

A second cause is a lack of international competitiveness. If a country has an overvalued exchange rate or high inflation, its exports may become expensive abroad and imports may look cheap at home, so export revenue may fall and import expenditure may rise. Low labour productivity from poor education and low investment could add to this. This is a structural deficit and is unlikely to correct itself.

Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss the consequences of a persistent current account deficit for a country.
ReminderCover domestic and external consequences. Then weigh them: does the cause and the size relative to GDP matter?

A persistent deficit means the country is spending more on imports and other payments than it earns, so residents consume more than the economy produces. It is likely to need to finance the gap by borrowing or by attracting foreign investment. That could lead to future outflows of interest, profits and dividends, which may worsen the primary income balance and the deficit further.

The falling net exports reduce aggregate demand, which can slow economic growth and raise unemployment, particularly in export and import-competing industries. Overseas lenders may lose confidence, which could put downward pressure on the exchange rate.

However, how serious this is depends on the cause and the size. A deficit caused by a boom in investment imports may be temporary and productive. It also matters as a percentage of GDP: a deficit of 2% of GDP is easily financed, whereas 8% may be unsustainable. Overall, a persistent structural deficit is likely to harm the economy over time, while a small cyclical one is of little concern.

Q6 Assess 12 marks Assess whether a current account surplus is always beneficial for a country.
ReminderOpen each paragraph with the question's wording. Consider causes of surplus. A surplus from recession is not a good one.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A current account surplus occurs when the credit items in trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income are greater than the debit items, so more money flows into the country than leaves it.

A current account surplus can be beneficial for a country because it may show firms are competitive and earning more from abroad than residents spend on imports. Higher export revenue may increase aggregate demand, which can raise output and employment, and the country builds up claims on the rest of the world that bring in future investment income. Switzerland, with a surplus on goods and investment income, is likely to benefit from the extra money coming in. This holds when the surplus arises from structural advantages such as high productivity, low inflation and strong investment.

A current account surplus is not always beneficial because it can come from a weak economy or create other problems. If it arises because a recession has cut spending on imports, residents' living standards may be falling, so it is not a good outcome. A large surplus may mean residents are consuming less than they produce, giving up current living standards, and the high demand for exports with extra money supply may cause inflation. Other countries may also put pressure on the country to change its policies, for example by letting its exchange rate appreciate.

Overall, a surplus is beneficial when it reflects strong competitiveness, but it is not beneficial when it comes from a recession or is so large that it reduces residents' consumption and provokes trade disputes. A judgement therefore needs the cause of the surplus, its size as a percentage of GDP and the state of the economy.

Top tips

  • Credits bring money in, debits take money out. Say it as you work through a table.
  • Exports minus imports. Always in that order, and keep the minus sign for a deficit.
  • Build the answer in stages. Goods, services, trade in goods and services, then primary and secondary income.
  • Value, not quantity. A trade deficit means import value exceeds export value.
  • The surplus causes are the reverse of the deficit causes. Learn one set and you know both.
  • Separate cyclical from structural. Structural deficits are the ones to worry about.
  • Judge size as a percentage of GDP. It shows how significant the imbalance is.
  • Evaluate a surplus too. It is not always good news, especially if caused by recession.
