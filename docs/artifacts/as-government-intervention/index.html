Methods and Effects of Government Intervention

Indirect taxes, subsidies, direct provision, maximum and minimum prices, buffer stocks and information. How each method works, who gains, who loses, and how to evaluate it.

The big idea

When markets fail, or when governments want a different outcome, they intervene. Each method changes prices, quantities and who pays. For every method ask four questions: What does it do to the market? Who gains? Who loses? What does it cost the government? Then evaluate how well it works.

1 · Specific indirect taxes

A specific indirect tax is a fixed amount per unit (for example $5 per litre), collected from the producer or seller but paid, in part or in full, by consumers through higher prices. The tax shifts the supply curve upwards (to the left) by the amount of the tax.

Impact

  • Price paid by consumers rises.
  • Quantity traded falls.
  • Price received by producers (after paying the tax) falls.
  • Government revenue = tax per unit × quantity sold.

Incidence

Tax incidence is how the burden is shared between consumers and producers. The consumer share is the rise in the market price; the producer share is the tax per unit minus that rise.

Consumer share = demand slope ÷ (demand slope + supply slope)

In practice: the less elastic side of the market pays more. Inelastic demand means consumers bear most of the tax. Inelastic supply means producers do.

Why use them? To raise revenue, to reduce consumption of demerit goods, and to make consumers face external costs. Limits: if demand is inelastic the tax barely reduces consumption; they are regressive; they may encourage smuggling and illegal markets; and the right tax level is hard to calculate.

2 · Subsidies

A subsidy is a payment by government to producers (or sometimes consumers) per unit or to support a good or service. A per-unit subsidy shifts supply downwards (to the right).

Impact

  • Market price falls; quantity rises.
  • Producers receive the lower market price plus the subsidy.
  • The benefit is shared in the same way as a tax burden: less elastic side gains more.
  • Cost to government = subsidy per unit × quantity.

Evaluate

  • Opportunity cost: the money could be spent elsewhere.
  • Needs tax revenue or borrowing.
  • Can keep inefficient firms alive.
  • Helps encourage merit goods and protects domestic industries or low-income buyers.
  • Hard to remove once introduced.

3 · Direct provision of goods and services

Governments provide some goods and services themselves, often free at the point of use and paid for out of taxation. Typical examples: public goods (street lighting, national defence, which are non-excludable and non-rival, so private firms will not supply them) and merit goods (education, healthcare, which are under-consumed if left to the market).

For: universal access, fixes missing markets, can improve equity. Against: high cost, rising demand, possible waste or poor quality without competition, and the opportunity cost of the funds.

4 · Maximum prices (price ceilings)

A legal limit on how high the price can go. To have any effect it must be set below the equilibrium price. Quantity demanded rises, quantity supplied falls, so there is a shortage.

  • Aims: keep essentials affordable (rent, staple foods, utilities).
  • Consequences: shortages and queues, rationing, black markets at higher prices, lower quality, suppliers leave the market.

5 · Minimum prices (price floors)

A legal limit on how low the price can go. To have any effect it must be set above the equilibrium price. Quantity supplied rises, quantity demanded falls, so there is a surplus.

  • Aims: support producers' incomes (agriculture), set a minimum wage, discourage consumption of some goods.
  • Consequences: surplus that someone must buy or store, cost to government if it buys the surplus, higher prices for consumers, and possible dumping of the surplus abroad.

6 · Buffer stock schemes

A government agency sets a price band, a minimum and a maximum price, for a commodity whose supply varies from harvest to harvest. When the price would fall below the minimum, the agency buys and stores the surplus. When it would rise above the maximum, it sells from the stock.

  • Aim: stabilise prices and farmers' incomes.
  • Problems: storage costs, wastage, the scheme can run out of stock in a run of bad harvests, the floor may be set too high and the stock builds without limit, and it needs funds up front. It is hard to set the "right" price band.

7 · Provision of information

Where people lack information, they make poor choices. Governments can require labelling, run campaigns and put health warnings on packaging.

For: cheap compared with other methods, improves decisions, respects choice. Against: may be ignored, effects slow and hard to measure, advertising by firms can drown it out, and it does little for addictive goods used by people who are already well informed.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

An import tax on vegetable oil (India)

The government of India planned an extra 5% tax on vegetable oil imports, with the extra revenue used to boost domestic oilseed production. Vegetable oil is a staple ingredient, so demand is relatively inelastic: consumers would bear a large share of the tax.

Exam use: an example of an indirect tax with two aims, revenue and protecting domestic producers. Link inelastic demand to incidence, and note the tax is regressive because poor households spend a larger share of income on it.

Taxes on sugary drinks

Several countries tax sugary soft drinks. Part of the response comes from firms reformulating their products rather than consumers cutting purchases, and the tax can raise money for health or education.

Exam use: shows an indirect tax used to correct a demerit good. Evaluate with elasticity: how much will demand fall? Mention that some of the incidence falls on producers.

Rent controls

In some cities, governments cap rents. Tenants in controlled homes benefit, but landlords supply fewer properties or reduce maintenance, and people searching for housing face shortages and may pay extra informally.

Exam use: a standard maximum price example. Draw the shortage and discuss rationing, black markets and quality.

Fuel subsidies

Governments in a number of countries subsidise fuel to keep transport costs and living costs low. The cost to the government can be very large, and it takes funds away from other spending. Removing the subsidy can be politically hard.

Exam use: a subsidy example for opportunity cost and for the difficulty of withdrawing support.

Staple crop price support and buffer stocks

Several governments buy staple crops such as rice or wheat at a guaranteed price and store them. Schemes with a floor set too high have built up large, costly stocks that had to be sold off or stored at great expense.

Exam use: use it to explain how a minimum price causes a surplus and why a buffer stock needs a realistic price band.

State-funded healthcare and education

Many governments provide these services free at the point of use, funded by tax. This gives wide access, but demand can outstrip supply, creating waiting times.

Exam use: direct provision of merit goods. Evaluate with opportunity cost and the possibility of inefficiency without competition.

Health warnings and food labelling

Warnings on tobacco packaging and nutritional labels on food are information provision. They are cheap, but their effect depends on whether people notice and act on them, and addictive products may be less responsive.

Exam use: a low-cost alternative to taxes. Compare it with an indirect tax in a "which is best" question.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by the incidence of a tax?
ReminderTwo marks, two ideas: what it is, and between whom.
Tax incidence is the way the burden of a tax is shared between consumers, who pay a higher price, and producers, who receive a lower price net of tax.
Q2 Calculate 4 marks A $4 per unit tax raises the price from $20 to $23 and cuts quantity from 100 to 90. Calculate (a) the consumers' share of the tax per unit and (b) the government's tax revenue.
ReminderUse the new quantity for tax revenue. The producer's share is what is left of the tax after the consumer's share.
(a) Consumers pay $23 − $20 = $3 more per unit, so $3 of the $4 tax falls on consumers (75%). Producers bear the remaining $1 per unit. (b) Tax revenue = $4 × 90 = $360. Revenue is tax per unit multiplied by the quantity sold after the tax.
Common mistakeMultiplying the tax by the old quantity (100). That gives $400, which is wrong.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain why a maximum price set above the equilibrium price has no effect.
ReminderA diagram helps: draw the ceiling above the equilibrium. Then explain in words.
A maximum price is a legal ceiling that stops the price rising above a given level. If it is set above the equilibrium price, the market price is already below the ceiling. Quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at the equilibrium, so no shortage is likely to appear. For example, a rent cap of $900 where the equilibrium rent is $700 is unlikely to have any effect because rents may never reach $900.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the likely effects of a maximum price on rented homes.
ReminderBuild a chain: ceiling below equilibrium → demand rises, supply falls → shortage → consequences. Use context: tenants, landlords, quality.

A maximum rent is a legal limit on rents set below the market equilibrium. At the lower rent, more households want to rent, so quantity demanded increases, while landlords find renting less profitable, so quantity supplied is likely to fall. The result may be a shortage of rented homes.

Tenants who find a controlled home benefit from the lower rent, but others may be unable to find homes and could have to pay unofficial extra payments in a black market. Landlords may spend less on maintenance or sell their properties, which could lower quality and supply further.

Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether a buffer stock scheme is an effective way to stabilise the price of a crop.
ReminderTwo sides. Explain how it works first, then give arguments for and against and finish with a conclusion.

A buffer stock scheme sets a minimum and a maximum price. The agency buys the crop when the price would fall below the minimum and sells from stock when it would rise above the maximum. After a good harvest supply rises and the price would fall, so buying lifts it back to the floor; after a poor harvest it sells stock to hold the price down. This can reduce price fluctuations and so may protect farmers' incomes and consumers from price spikes.

However, the scheme has problems. Storage is costly and some crops spoil, and the agency needs funds to buy. If a run of poor harvests exhausts the stock, it may no longer be able to hold the price down. If the minimum price is set above the long-run equilibrium, the agency may have to keep buying and the stock could grow without limit. Overall, a scheme can work if the price band is realistic and reserves are adequate, but it is only as effective as the accuracy of that price band.

Q6 Assess 12 marks Assess whether a government should use an indirect tax rather than provide information to reduce consumption of sugary drinks.
ReminderOpen each paragraph with the question's wording. Compare the two methods, not just describe them. End with a judgement that says what the answer depends on.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

An indirect tax is a tax on spending that raises the price of the good, whereas information provision gives consumers facts, such as labels or campaigns, so they can choose differently.

A government should use an indirect tax rather than provide information to reduce consumption of sugary drinks if it needs a reliable fall in quantity demanded. A tax shifts supply upwards, which is likely to raise the price consumers pay and so reduce quantity demanded along the demand curve; it also raises revenue that could fund health spending. If shoppers can switch to water or sugar-free versions, demand is likely to be more elastic and the fall in consumption could be larger. However, if demand is inelastic because of habit, consumption may barely fall and the burden could land mainly on consumers, particularly low-income households, which could make the tax regressive.

A government should provide information rather than use a tax if it wants to change behaviour without raising prices. Labels and campaigns can correct information gaps so consumers may value the long-term health costs more highly, which could shift demand to the left. Health campaigns in schools could shape habits early. But the effect may be slow, hard to measure, and could be swamped by advertising from producers, so it may not cut consumption by much on its own.

Overall, the tax is likely to be more effective in the short run because it changes the price signal directly, particularly if demand is elastic, while information helps in the long run and costs less. The best approach is probably a combination of the two, and the right choice depends on the elasticity of demand, the level of the tax and how well informed consumers are already.

Top tips

  • Label diagrams fully. Axes, S₁ and S₂, D, equilibrium prices and quantities, and shade the tax revenue or the shortage.
  • Shift the right curve. Taxes and subsidies shift supply. Price controls are horizontal lines that do not shift a curve.
  • Check the side of equilibrium. A maximum price only works below equilibrium; a minimum price only works above it.
  • For tax revenue, use the new quantity. Tax per unit × quantity sold after the tax.
  • Say who bears the tax and why. Link it to the elasticities of demand and supply.
  • Include the government's cost. Subsidies and buying surplus stock cost money with an opportunity cost.
  • Evaluate every method. Elasticity, unintended consequences, cost, time lags, alternatives.
  • Use specific context. Rents, fuel, rice, tobacco, healthcare. It shows application.
